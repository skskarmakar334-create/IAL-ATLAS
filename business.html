01 — What is a business?

A business combines resources to produce goods or services that meet customer needs and wants. It may aim to earn profit, grow, survive or achieve social goals.

Inputs → Business Process → Outputs

Inputs may include labour, materials, finance, land and enterprise. Outputs are goods or services produced for customers.

02 — Revenue, costs and profit

Revenue = Selling Price × Quantity Sold
Total Cost = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs
Profit = Total Revenue − Total Costs

Fixed costs generally do not change directly with output in the short term. Total variable costs change as output changes.

03 — Break-even analysis

Break-even is the output level at which total revenue equals total costs, so there is no profit or loss.

Contribution per Unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per Unit
Break-even Output = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per Unit

Example: fixed costs = $2,000, price = $20 and variable cost = $12. Contribution = $8, so break-even output = 2,000 ÷ 8 = 250 units.

04 — Cash flow

Cash flow tracks money entering and leaving a business. A profitable business can still face cash-flow problems if customers pay late or bills become due before cash arrives.

Net Cash Flow = Cash Inflows − Cash Outflows
Closing Balance = Opening Balance + Net Cash Flow

05 — The marketing mix

The traditional 4Ps help a business develop a marketing strategy.

  • Product: what is offered to customers.
  • Price: what customers pay.
  • Place: where and how the product is sold.
  • Promotion: how customers learn about it.

Effective marketing aligns these decisions with the target market and the business's objectives.

06 — Market research

Market research helps businesses understand customers, demand, competitors and market conditions.

  • Primary research: data collected directly, such as surveys or interviews.
  • Secondary research: existing information, such as published reports or official statistics.

Primary research can be tailored to a specific question, while secondary research may be quicker or cheaper to obtain.

07 — Productivity

Productivity measures output relative to the resources used to produce it.

Labour Productivity = Output ÷ Number of Workers

Productivity may improve through training, better equipment, improved processes or effective motivation.

08 — Business ownership

Different legal structures affect control, finance, liability and decision-making.

  • Sole trader: owned by one person; usually has unlimited liability.
  • Partnership: owned by partners under an agreed arrangement.
  • Limited company: legally separate from its owners; shareholders generally have limited liability.

The best structure depends on the business's size, goals, funding needs and legal environment.

09 — Motivation

Motivation is the willingness of people to put effort into their work. It can be influenced by pay, recognition, responsibility, job security, working conditions and opportunities for development.

Different employees respond to different incentives, so managers should consider context rather than assuming one method works for everyone.

10 — Stakeholders

Stakeholders are people or groups affected by business decisions.

  • Owners may want profit and growth.
  • Employees may want fair pay and job security.
  • Customers may want quality and value.
  • Suppliers may want reliable payments.
  • Government may expect legal compliance and tax payments.

Stakeholder interests can conflict, requiring managers to make informed trade-offs.
