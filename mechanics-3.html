M3 • SECTION 01

Further Kinematics

M3 extends the constant-acceleration model by allowing acceleration, velocity and displacement to be related through functions of time, position and other variables.

Core idea: When acceleration is not constant, the standard SUVAT equations cannot automatically be used. Instead, use the definitions of velocity and acceleration together with differentiation, integration and the chain rule.

Lesson 1 — The Fundamental Definitions

For one-dimensional motion, displacement x is measured from a chosen origin. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement, while acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.

v = dx/dt
a = dv/dt = d²x/dt²

These equations are the starting point for almost every variable acceleration problem.

Lesson 2 — Integrating Acceleration

If acceleration is known as a function of time, integrate to obtain velocity. A constant of integration must then be determined from the initial conditions.

a = dv/dt
v = ∫a dt

Then use

v = dx/dt

and integrate again when displacement is required.

Reliable method

  1. Write a = dv/dt.
  2. Integrate with respect to t.
  3. Use a known value of v to find the constant.
  4. Write v = dx/dt if x is required.
  5. Integrate again.
  6. Use the displacement condition to determine the second constant.

WORKED EXAMPLE 01

A particle moves in a straight line with acceleration a = 6t + 4. At t = 0 its velocity is 3 m/s. Find its velocity at t = 5 s.

a = dv/dt

dv/dt = 6t + 4

Integrating:

v = 3t² + 4t + C

At t = 0, v = 3, so C = 3.

Therefore:

v = 3t² + 4t + 3

At t = 5:

v = 75 + 20 + 3

v = 98 m/s

Lesson 3 — Finding Displacement from a(t)

If the particle's initial displacement is known, integration may be performed twice.

WORKED EXAMPLE 02

A particle has acceleration a = 12t − 6. Initially it has velocity 5 m/s and displacement 2 m. Find x in terms of t.

a = dv/dt

dv/dt = 12t − 6

v = 6t² − 6t + C

At t = 0, v = 5, so C = 5.

Thus:

v = 6t² − 6t + 5

Since v = dx/dt:

x = 2t³ − 3t² + 5t + D

At t = 0, x = 2, so D = 2.

x = 2t³ − 3t² + 5t + 2

Lesson 4 — Acceleration as a Function of Displacement

A particularly important M3 technique occurs when acceleration is given in terms of displacement rather than time.

Since

v = dx/dt

the chain rule gives

a = dv/dt = dv/dx × dx/dt

Therefore:

a = v dv/dx

This allows time to be eliminated from the problem.

WORKED EXAMPLE 03

A particle has acceleration a = 4x. Its velocity is 3 m/s when x = 2. Find v² in terms of x.

a = v dv/dx

v dv/dx = 4x

v dv = 4x dx

Integrating:

½v² = 2x² + C

Therefore:

v² = 4x² + C'

When x = 2 and v = 3:

9 = 16 + C'

C' = −7

v² = 4x² − 7

Lesson 5 — Useful Kinematic Identities

a = v dv/dx
v = dx/dt
a = d²x/dt²

Choose the form that removes the variable you do not need. If the question gives a(t), work with t. If it gives a(x), the chain-rule form is usually more efficient.

Common trap: Do not use SUVAT simply because a question involves acceleration. SUVAT requires constant acceleration. If acceleration is variable, check the information before selecting a method.

M3 • SECTION 02

Elastic Strings & Springs

This section develops the mathematical model of elastic strings and springs, including Hooke's law, modulus, energy, equilibrium and oscillatory motion.

Lesson 1 — Extension and Natural Length

Let l be the natural length and L be the current length. The extension x is

x = L − l

Extension is not the same as the total length. This distinction is essential in M3 questions.

Lesson 2 — Hooke's Law for a Spring

For an ideal spring obeying Hooke's law, the magnitude of the force is proportional to its extension or compression.

F = kx

where k is the spring stiffness.

For a string, the standard elastic model is expressed using the modulus λ.

T = λx/l

where:

  • T is the tension.
  • λ is the modulus.
  • x is the extension.
  • l is the natural length.

WORKED EXAMPLE 04

An elastic string has natural length 2.5 m and modulus 80 N. It is stretched to a length of 3.0 m. Find the tension.

Extension:

x = 3.0 − 2.5 = 0.5 m

Using:

T = λx/l

T = 80(0.5)/2.5

T = 16 N

Lesson 3 — Elastic Potential Energy

The work done in stretching an elastic string is stored as elastic potential energy.

E = ½λx²/l

For a spring obeying F = kx:

E = ½kx²

The energy may also be interpreted as the area under a force-extension graph.

WORKED EXAMPLE 05

An elastic string has modulus 100 N and natural length 2 m. It is extended by 0.4 m. Find the elastic energy stored.

E = ½λx²/l

E = ½ × 100 × 0.4² / 2

E = 4 J

Elastic potential energy = 4 J

Lesson 4 — Elastic Strings in Equilibrium

When an elastic string supports a particle, its tension can be balanced against weight or other forces.

Vertical equilibrium method

  1. Draw the particle and identify the direction of tension.
  2. Write the extension from the natural length.
  3. Use the elastic law to calculate T.
  4. Apply Newton's second law or equilibrium.
  5. Only then solve for the required variable.
T = mg

for a particle hanging at rest from a single vertical elastic string.

Lesson 5 — Energy in Elastic Systems

When a particle moves while attached to an elastic string or spring, energy can transfer between gravitational potential energy, kinetic energy and elastic potential energy.

Total mechanical energy = KE + GPE + elastic energy

If no non-conservative work is done, the total remains constant.

Exam trap: If a string is slack, it cannot exert a compressive force. The elastic-string model only applies while the string is taut and within the stated elastic range.

M3 • SECTION 03

Further Dynamics & SHM

M3 dynamics introduces variable forces, gravitation and simple harmonic motion, requiring a strong connection between Newton's second law and differential equations.

Lesson 1 — Newton's Second Law

The fundamental equation remains

F = ma

but the acceleration may now depend on displacement, velocity or time. The correct positive direction must be chosen before resolving forces.

Lesson 2 — Variable Forces

If a force varies with displacement, Newton's second law may be combined with

a = v dv/dx

to obtain a relationship between velocity and position.

Variable-force strategy

  1. Choose a positive direction.
  2. Write the resultant force.
  3. Use F = ma.
  4. Replace a with v dv/dx if the force depends on x.
  5. Integrate.
  6. Apply the given initial condition.

Lesson 3 — Newton's Law of Gravitation

Two point masses attract one another with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.

F = GMm/r²

Here G is the universal gravitational constant and r is the distance between the centres of the two masses.

Lesson 4 — Gravitational Field Strength

The gravitational field strength at distance r from a point mass M is

g = GM/r²

Therefore the gravitational force on a mass m is

F = mg = GMm/r²

WORKED EXAMPLE 06

A satellite of mass m is at distance r from the centre of a planet of mass M. Show that its orbital speed for a circular orbit is

v = √(GM/r)

For circular motion:

GMm/r² = mv²/r

Cancel m:

GM/r² = v²/r

Multiply by r:

v² = GM/r

Therefore:

v = √(GM/r)

Lesson 5 — Definition of Simple Harmonic Motion

A particle performs simple harmonic motion if its acceleration is proportional to its displacement from a fixed point and is always directed towards that point.

If x is measured from the equilibrium position, then

a = −ω²x

or

d²x/dt² = −ω²x

The negative sign is essential: it indicates that acceleration is directed towards the equilibrium position.

Lesson 6 — SHM Displacement

A general displacement equation may be written as

x = A cos(ωt + φ)

or equivalently using a sine form. A is the amplitude and φ is the phase constant.

The amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium.

Lesson 7 — SHM Velocity

A very useful velocity-displacement relationship is

v² = ω²(A² − x²)

Therefore the maximum speed occurs at x = 0:

vmax = ωA

At an extreme position x = ±A, the velocity is zero.

Lesson 8 — SHM Acceleration

a = −ω²x

Hence maximum acceleration occurs at maximum displacement:

amax = ω²A

Lesson 9 — Period and Frequency

T = 2π/ω
f = 1/T = ω/(2π)

The period is the time for one complete oscillation.

WORKED EXAMPLE 07

A particle performs SHM with amplitude 0.25 m and angular frequency 8 rad/s. Find its maximum speed and maximum acceleration.

Maximum speed:

vmax = ωA

vmax = 8 × 0.25

vmax = 2 m/s

Maximum acceleration:

amax = ω²A

amax = 8² × 0.25

amax = 16 m/s²

Lesson 10 — SHM Energy

For SHM, total mechanical energy remains constant. The kinetic energy is greatest at equilibrium and zero at the extreme positions.

Etotal = ½mω²A²

At displacement x:

KE = ½mω²(A² − x²)
PE = ½mω²x²
SHM trap: The condition is not merely that the particle oscillates. You must establish that acceleration is proportional to displacement and directed towards the equilibrium position.

M3 • SECTION 04

Motion in a Circle

Circular-motion problems require careful resolution towards the centre of the circle. The radial equation is one of the most important tools in this part of M3.

Lesson 1 — Angular Speed

ω = v/r

For one complete revolution:

v = 2πr/T

so

ω = 2π/T

Lesson 2 — Radial Acceleration

A particle moving in a circle has an acceleration directed towards the centre.

a = v²/r
a = rω²

Therefore the resultant inward force must satisfy

F = mv²/r = mrω²

WORKED EXAMPLE 08

A particle of mass 2 kg moves in a circle of radius 1.5 m at speed 6 m/s. Find the resultant radial force.

F = mv²/r

F = 2 × 36 / 1.5

F = 48 N

Lesson 3 — Horizontal Circular Motion

For a horizontal circle, the vertical and horizontal force balances must be considered separately where appropriate.

For a conical pendulum, the tension has:

  • a vertical component balancing the weight;
  • a horizontal component providing the centripetal force.

Conical pendulum method

  1. Draw the tension along the string.
  2. Resolve vertically: vertical component balances mg.
  3. Resolve horizontally towards the centre.
  4. Set the horizontal resultant equal to mv²/r.
  5. Use the geometry of the cone.

Lesson 4 — Vertical Circular Motion

For a vertical circle, gravity changes its component relative to the radial direction as the particle moves.

At every point:

Resultant inward force = mv²/r

Energy conservation is often used to relate speeds at different points of the circle.

WORKED EXAMPLE 09

A particle moves in a vertical circle of radius r. At the lowest point its speed is v. Taking the highest point as 2r above the lowest point, find its speed V at the top if no energy is lost.

Loss of kinetic energy = gain in gravitational potential energy.

½mv² − ½mV² = mg(2r)

Cancel m and multiply by 2:

v² − V² = 4gr

Therefore:

V² = v² − 4gr

Direction trap: Centripetal acceleration is not an additional force. It is the acceleration caused by the resultant of the actual forces.

M3 • SECTION 05

Statics of Rigid Bodies

Rigid-body statics combines forces, moments, centres of mass and equilibrium. Diagram quality becomes especially important here.

Lesson 1 — Rigid Bodies

A rigid body is an idealised body whose shape does not change under the forces being considered.

Unlike a particle, a rigid body can experience rotational effects, so moments must be considered.

Lesson 2 — Moment of a Force

The moment of a force about a point measures its turning effect.

Moment = Force × perpendicular distance

The unit is N m.

A clockwise and anticlockwise sign convention should be chosen and used consistently.

WORKED EXAMPLE 10

A 30 N force acts perpendicular to a rod at a distance of 0.8 m from a pivot. Find its moment.

Moment = Fd

= 30 × 0.8

= 24 N m

Lesson 3 — Equilibrium of a Rigid Body

For a rigid body to remain in equilibrium:

ΣFx = 0
ΣFy = 0
ΣM = 0

These conditions ensure there is neither translational nor rotational acceleration.

Lesson 4 — Choosing a Point for Moments

Take moments about a point that eliminates as many unknown forces as possible.

Exam technique

  1. Draw the complete force diagram.
  2. Resolve horizontal and vertical forces.
  3. Choose a convenient moment point.
  4. Write clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments.
  5. Use the force equations to determine remaining unknowns.

Lesson 5 — Centre of Mass

The centre of mass is the point at which the mass of a body may be considered to be concentrated when considering translational motion and gravitational effects.

For a uniform rod, the centre of mass lies at its midpoint.

For composite systems, moments about a convenient origin can be used to determine the centre of mass.

x̄ = Σ(mx)/Σm

WORKED EXAMPLE 11

Two particles of masses 2 kg and 6 kg are positioned at x = 1 m and x = 5 m respectively. Find the x-coordinate of their centre of mass.

x̄ = Σ(mx)/Σm

x̄ = (2×1 + 6×5)/(2+6)

x̄ = 32/8

x̄ = 4 m

Lesson 6 — Ladders and Contact Forces

For a ladder or rod resting against surfaces, identify whether contacts are smooth or rough.

  • A smooth contact provides a normal reaction only.
  • A rough contact may provide both normal reaction and friction.
  • The weight acts through the centre of mass.
  • Moments should be taken about a point that removes unknown reactions.
Moment trap: The distance used in a moment is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force — not necessarily the physical distance to the point where the force is applied.

M3 • EXAM METHODS

How to Attack M3 Problems

Variable Kinematics

  1. Identify whether the acceleration depends on t, x or another variable.
  2. Use a = dv/dt for time-based acceleration.
  3. Use a = v dv/dx when displacement is involved.
  4. Integrate carefully.
  5. Apply initial conditions immediately after integration.

Elastic Systems

  1. Find natural length and extension separately.
  2. Use T = λx/l or F = kx.
  3. Check whether the string is taut.
  4. Use Newton's laws for force questions.
  5. Use energy when several positions and speeds are involved.

SHM

  1. Measure x from the equilibrium position.
  2. Establish that a is proportional to −x.
  3. Identify ω² from the acceleration equation.
  4. Use T = 2π/ω.
  5. Use v² = ω²(A² − x²) for speed at a given displacement.

Circular Motion

  1. Draw the circle.
  2. Mark the centre.
  3. Choose the inward direction as positive for the radial equation.
  4. Resolve actual forces towards the centre.
  5. Set the resultant equal to mv²/r.
  6. Use energy separately when heights change.

Rigid Bodies

  1. Draw every force.
  2. Resolve horizontally and vertically.
  3. Choose a convenient pivot.
  4. Use perpendicular distances for moments.
  5. Apply ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0.
Universal M3 rule: Never start calculating before deciding what physical model the question is using. A large proportion of M3 errors occur because the correct mathematics is applied to the wrong physical quantity.

M3 • COMBINED PRACTICE

Exam-Style Practice

These questions deliberately combine methods from different parts of M3.

1. A particle has acceleration a = 8t − 4. Its initial velocity is 6 m/s. Find its velocity when t = 3.
Show answer

Integrate: v = 4t² − 4t + C. At t = 0, C = 6. At t = 3: v = 30 m/s.

2. A particle has acceleration a = 6x and velocity 2 m/s when x = 1. Find v² in terms of x.
Show answer

v dv/dx = 6x. Hence v² = 6x² + C. Using x = 1, v² = 4, so C = −2. v² = 6x² − 2.

3. An elastic string has natural length 3 m, modulus 90 N and extension 0.6 m. Find its tension and elastic energy.
Show answer

T = 90(0.6)/3 = 18 N. E = ½(90)(0.6²)/3 = 5.4 J.

4. A particle performs SHM with amplitude 0.4 m and angular frequency 5 rad/s. Find its maximum speed.
Show answer

vmax = ωA = 5(0.4) = 2 m/s.

5. A particle moves in a circle of radius 3 m at 9 m/s. Find its radial acceleration.
Show answer

a = v²/r = 81/3 = 27 m/s².

6. A 12 N force acts perpendicular to a rod at a distance of 2.5 m from a pivot. Find its moment.
Show answer

M = Fd = 12(2.5) = 30 N m.

7. A satellite moves in a circular orbit of radius r around a planet of mass M. Derive its orbital speed.
Show answer

GMm/r² = mv²/r. Therefore: v = √(GM/r).

8. A particle has SHM equation x = 0.3 cos(4t). State its amplitude, angular frequency and period.
Show answer

A = 0.3 m, ω = 4 rad/s, T = 2π/4 = π/2 s.

9. Two particles of masses 3 kg and 5 kg are at x = 2 m and x = 8 m respectively. Find their centre of mass.
Show answer

x̄ = (3×2 + 5×8)/8 = 46/8. x̄ = 5.75 m.

10. A particle moves in a vertical circle. Explain why both Newton's second law and conservation of energy may be needed.
Show answer

Newton's second law gives the radial force condition ΣF = mv²/r, while energy conservation can relate speeds at different heights.

Before moving to the 100-question exam
  • Can you derive a = v dv/dx?
  • Can you distinguish natural length from current length?
  • Can you recognise SHM from an acceleration equation?
  • Can you construct a radial equation?
  • Can you take moments using perpendicular distances?
  • Can you use several M3 ideas in the same problem?
