Work & Energy

A complete Edexcel International A Level Mechanics 2 guide to work, kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy, energy conservation, resistance, power, efficiency and energy methods.

½mv² KINETIC ENERGY

LESSON 01

What Is Work?

In mechanics, work is done when a force causes a displacement. Work measures the amount of energy transferred by a force.

Core definition

The work done by a force is the product of the force and the displacement in the direction of the force.

Work is a scalar quantity. It has magnitude but no direction. Its SI unit is the joule, J.

W = Fs when the force and displacement are in the same direction

What does one joule mean?

One joule is the work done when a force of 1 N causes a displacement of 1 m in the direction of the force.

1 J = 1 N m
Exam idea

Do not automatically use W = Fs. First check whether the force is parallel to the displacement. If it is at an angle, the component of the force in the direction of motion must be used.

LESSON 02

Work Done by a Force at an Angle

Suppose a constant force F acts at an angle θ to the displacement s. Only the component of the force parallel to the displacement does work.

The component of F in the direction of motion is:

F cosθ

Therefore:

W = Fs cosθ

Special cases

Angle cosθ Work
θ = 0° 1 W = Fs — maximum positive work
θ = 90° 0 W = 0 — no work
θ = 180° -1 W = -Fs — negative work

Positive work

If the force has a component in the direction of displacement, it transfers energy to the particle. The work is positive.

Negative work

If the force has a component opposite to the displacement, the force removes mechanical energy from the particle. The work is negative.

Zero work

A force perpendicular to the displacement does no work. This is particularly important for the normal reaction on a particle moving along a straight fixed surface.

Common trap

A force can be large and still do zero work. For example, if a reaction force is perpendicular to the displacement, its work is zero.

LESSON 03

Work Done by Weight

The weight of a particle acts vertically downward with magnitude mg. The work done by weight depends on the change in vertical position.

Work done by weight = mg × vertical distance moved downward

If the particle moves vertically upward through height h, weight acts opposite to the displacement:

Wweight = -mgh

If the particle moves vertically downward through height h:

Wweight = +mgh

Connection with gravitational potential energy

When a particle rises, its gravitational potential energy increases. That energy comes from work done against gravity.

ΔGPE = mgh

Therefore:

Work done by gravity = -ΔGPE
Useful viewpoint

Moving upward: gravity does negative work. Moving downward: gravity does positive work.

LESSON 04

Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy associated with the motion of a particle. A particle of mass m moving with speed v has kinetic energy:

KE = ½mv²

Kinetic energy is always non-negative because speed is squared.

Important consequences

  • Doubling speed makes kinetic energy four times as large.
  • Tripling speed makes kinetic energy nine times as large.
  • Doubling mass doubles kinetic energy at the same speed.
  • A particle at rest has zero kinetic energy.

Change in kinetic energy

If the initial speed is u and final speed is v:

ΔKE = ½mv² − ½mu²

or:

ΔKE = ½m(v² − u²)

LESSON 05

Gravitational Potential Energy

Gravitational potential energy depends on vertical position relative to a chosen reference level, called a datum.

GPE = mgh

Here:

  • m = mass in kg
  • g = acceleration due to gravity in m s⁻²
  • h = vertical height above the chosen datum

The datum

The zero level for gravitational potential energy can be chosen conveniently. The important quantity in energy equations is usually the change in height, not the absolute value of GPE.

Energy difference

If a particle moves from height h₁ to height h₂, the change in gravitational potential energy is:

ΔGPE = mg(h₂ − h₁)
Exam trap

On an inclined plane, h is the vertical height change, NOT the distance travelled along the slope.

LESSON 06

The Work–Energy Principle

The work done by the resultant force on a particle is equal to its change in kinetic energy.

Wresultant = ΔKE
Therefore:

Wresultant = ½mv² − ½mu²

What does resultant work mean?

Every force acting on the particle may do work. The total work is the algebraic sum of the work done by all forces.

ΣW = ΔKE

For example, if a particle is pulled by a force P while resistance R acts against its motion:

Ps − Rs = ½mv² − ½mu²

This is often much faster than resolving forces and using Newton's second law.

Energy-method trigger

If the question gives forces, distances, speeds, heights or resistance and asks for an unknown speed/distance/force, consider whether an energy equation can connect the initial and final states.

LESSON 07

Conservation of Mechanical Energy

Mechanical energy consists of kinetic and gravitational potential energy:

Mechanical energy = KE + GPE

If there is no energy transfer to resistance or another external system:

KE + GPE = constant
Therefore:

½mu² + mgh₁ = ½mv² + mgh₂

The mass can often be cancelled:

½u² + gh₁ = ½v² + gh₂

Physical meaning

As a particle moves upward, gravitational potential energy increases. If no energy is lost, this increase comes from kinetic energy, so the particle slows down.

As a particle moves downward, gravitational potential energy decreases. That energy becomes kinetic energy, so the particle speeds up.

LESSON 08

Energy with Resistance

Resistance is a non-conservative force. It transfers mechanical energy into other forms, usually thermal/internal energy.

If a constant resistance R acts over distance s:

Energy transferred against resistance = Rs

For a particle moving from state 1 to state 2:

Initial KE + Initial GPE = Final KE + Final GPE + Energy lost to resistance
Therefore:

½mu² + mgh₁ = ½mv² + mgh₂ + Rs

Alternative work equation

You can also use work-energy:

Total work done = change in KE

For a particle pulled by force P against resistance R:

Ps − Rs + Wgravity = ½m(v² − u²)
Sign warning

Do not put Rs on the left as positive if resistance opposes the motion. Resistance removes mechanical energy.

LESSON 09

Energy on an Inclined Plane

Inclined-plane problems are especially suitable for energy methods because the vertical height change can be obtained geometrically.

If a particle moves a distance s along an incline at angle θ:

h = s sinθ

Therefore the change in gravitational potential energy is:

ΔGPE = mgs sinθ

Smooth incline

If the plane is smooth, there is no frictional energy loss. If the particle moves upward:

½mu² = ½mv² + mgsinθ × s
or:

½mu² = ½mv² + mgs sinθ

Rough incline

If resistance R acts along the plane:

½mu² = ½mv² + mgs sinθ + Rs
when the particle travels upward.

Remember

The normal reaction does no work when it is perpendicular to the particle's displacement along the plane.

LESSON 10

Stopping Distance

Suppose a particle is moving with speed u and is brought to rest by a constant resistance R.

Initial kinetic energy is:

½mu²

At the stopping point, final kinetic energy is zero. The resistance has removed all the initial kinetic energy:

½mu² = Rs
Therefore:

s = mu² / 2R

What does this tell us?

Stopping distance is proportional to the square of the initial speed when mass and resistance are constant.

s ∝ u²

So doubling the initial speed requires four times the stopping distance under the same constant resistance.

LESSON 11

Work Done from a Force–Displacement Graph

When force varies with displacement, W = Fs cannot generally be used with one constant force value.

The work done is the area under the force–displacement graph.

Work = signed area under an F–s graph

Common shapes

Rectangle

Area = base × height

Triangle

Area = ½ × base × height

Trapezium

Area = ½(a + b)h

Curve

Use the appropriate area method or integration if required.

Areas below the displacement axis represent negative work.

Important

Do not simply add all areas if some parts of the graph lie below the axis. Work is signed.

LESSON 12

Power

Power measures the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

P = W / t

The SI unit of power is the watt:

1 W = 1 J s⁻¹

Power when a force acts at constant velocity

Since:

W = Fs
and:

P = W/t
we obtain:

P = F(s/t)
Since s/t is velocity v:

P = Fv

This applies when the force and velocity are in the same direction.

General form

P = Fv cosθ

where θ is the angle between the force and velocity.

Average versus instantaneous power

Quantity Formula
Average power Pavg = total work / total time
Instantaneous power P = Fv cosθ

LESSON 13

Efficiency and Energy Transfer

Efficiency compares useful output energy with total input energy.

Efficiency = useful output energy / total input energy

As a percentage:

Efficiency = (useful output / total input) × 100%

The same relationship can be written using power:

Efficiency = useful output power / total input power × 100%

Energy cannot disappear

If a machine is less than 100% efficient, some input energy is transferred into unwanted forms such as thermal energy or sound.

Input energy = useful energy + wasted energy
Exam method

Keep the quantities in the same units before calculating efficiency. For example, do not divide a value in kJ by a value in J without converting first.

LESSON 14

Energy Methods: The Universal Workflow

Many Mechanics 2 problems become much easier if you treat the motion as a transfer of energy between clearly defined initial and final states.

Draw or inspect the diagram. Identify the initial and final positions.
Choose a datum. Pick a convenient zero height for gravitational potential energy.
List the initial energy. Usually ½mu² + mgh.
List the final energy. Usually ½mv² + mgh.
Identify energy losses. For constant resistance use Rs.
Include external work. A driving force doing work adds energy to the system.
Form the equation before substituting. This reduces sign errors.
Solve and check. Check units, signs and whether the answer is physically sensible.
Initial energy + energy supplied = final energy + energy dissipated

WORKED EXAMPLE 01

Constant Force Parallel to Motion

Question

A constant horizontal force of 18 N moves a particle through 7 m. Find the work done by the force.

Since the force is parallel to the displacement:

W = Fs

W = 18 × 7

W = 126 J

WORKED EXAMPLE 02

Force at an Angle

Question

A force of 30 N acts at 60° to the direction of motion of a particle. The particle moves 8 m. Find the work done by the force.

W = Fs cosθ

W = 30 × 8 × cos60°

W = 240 × 0.5

W = 120 J

WORKED EXAMPLE 03

Negative Work by Resistance

Question

A resistance of 14 N acts on a particle moving 12 m forwards. Find the work done by the resistance.

The resistance acts opposite to the displacement.

W = Fs cos180°

W = 14 × 12 × (-1)

W = −168 J

WORKED EXAMPLE 04

Change in Kinetic Energy

Question

A particle of mass 5 kg accelerates from 4 m/s to 10 m/s. Find the increase in kinetic energy.

ΔKE = ½m(v² − u²)

= ½ × 5 × (10² − 4²)

= 2.5 × (100 − 16)

= 2.5 × 84

ΔKE = 210 J

WORKED EXAMPLE 05

Work–Energy Principle

Question

A 3 kg particle initially moves at 5 m/s. A constant resultant force does 96 J of work on it. Find its final speed.

Work done = change in kinetic energy.

96 = ½ × 3 × (v² − 5²)

96 = 1.5(v² − 25)

64 = v² − 25

v² = 89

v = √89 ≈ 9.43 m/s

WORKED EXAMPLE 06

Vertical Lift

Question

A 4 kg particle is lifted vertically through 6 m at constant speed. Find the work done by the lifting force. Take g = 9.8 m/s².

Constant speed means there is no change in kinetic energy.

The lifting force balances the weight:

F = mg = 4 × 9.8 = 39.2 N

Work done = Fs

= 39.2 × 6

Work = 235.2 J

This is also exactly the increase in gravitational potential energy:

mgh = 4 × 9.8 × 6 = 235.2 J.

WORKED EXAMPLE 07

Speed from a Vertical Drop

Question

A particle is released from rest and falls through 5 m. Assuming no resistance, find its speed just before impact. Take g = 9.8 m/s².

Use conservation of mechanical energy:

Initial KE + initial GPE = final KE + final GPE.

0 + mgh = ½mv² + 0

mgh = ½mv²

gh = ½v²

v² = 2gh

v² = 2 × 9.8 × 5 = 98

v = √98 ≈ 9.90 m/s

WORKED EXAMPLE 08

Particle Moving Up a Smooth Incline

Question

A particle moves up a smooth plane inclined at 30° to the horizontal. Its initial speed is 14 m/s. Find the distance travelled before it comes to rest. Take g = 9.8 m/s².

At the highest point:

v = 0

Vertical height gained:

h = s sin30°

Conservation of energy gives:

½m(14²) = mgh

½(14²) = 9.8s sin30°

98 = 9.8s × 0.5

98 = 4.9s

s = 20 m

WORKED EXAMPLE 09

Rough Incline

Question

A particle of mass 2 kg moves up a rough plane inclined at 30°. The particle has initial speed 12 m/s and travels 5 m before its speed falls to 4 m/s. The resistance is constant. Find the resistance. Take g = 9.8 m/s².

Use:

Initial KE = final KE + gain in GPE + energy lost to resistance.

½(2)(12²) = ½(2)(4²) + (2)(9.8)(5 sin30°) + 5R

144 = 16 + 49 + 5R

144 = 65 + 5R

79 = 5R

R = 15.8 N

WORKED EXAMPLE 10

Stopping Distance

Question

A 1200 kg car travels at 20 m/s. A constant total resistance of 6000 N acts on it. Find its stopping distance.

At rest, final KE = 0.

Initial kinetic energy = work done against resistance.

½mu² = Rs

½ × 1200 × 20² = 6000s

240000 = 6000s

s = 40 m

WORKED EXAMPLE 11

Force–Displacement Graph

Question

A force increases uniformly from 0 N to 20 N while a particle moves through 6 m. Find the work done.

The F–s graph is a triangle.

Work = area under graph.

W = ½ × base × height

W = ½ × 6 × 20

W = 60 J

WORKED EXAMPLE 12

Average Power

Question

A motor transfers 18 000 J of useful energy in 12 s. Find its average useful power.

P = W/t

P = 18000 / 12

P = 1500 W = 1.50 kW

WORKED EXAMPLE 13

Instantaneous Power

Question

A driving force of 2500 N acts on a car travelling at 16 m/s. The force is parallel to the velocity. Find the power supplied.

P = Fv

P = 2500 × 16

P = 40 000 W = 40 kW

WORKED EXAMPLE 14

Power at an Angle

Question

A force of 80 N acts at 60° to the velocity of a particle moving at 5 m/s. Find the instantaneous power supplied by the force.

P = Fv cosθ

P = 80 × 5 × cos60°

P = 400 × 0.5

P = 200 W

WORKED EXAMPLE 15

Efficiency

Question

A machine receives 5000 J of energy and produces 3600 J of useful output energy. Find its efficiency.

Efficiency = useful output / input × 100%

= 3600 / 5000 × 100%

Efficiency = 72%

PRACTICE

Exam-Style Practice

1. A constant force of 25 N moves a particle 8 m in its direction. Find the work done.
Show answer

W = Fs = 25 × 8.

W = 200 J.

2. A force of 40 N acts at 60° to a displacement of 5 m. Find the work done.
Show answer

W = Fs cos60° = 40 × 5 × 0.5.

W = 100 J.

3. A 6 kg particle travels at 10 m/s. Find its kinetic energy.
Show answer

KE = ½ × 6 × 10².

KE = 300 J.

4. A 2 kg particle rises vertically through 7 m. Find its increase in GPE using g = 9.8 m/s².
Show answer

ΔGPE = mgh = 2 × 9.8 × 7.

ΔGPE = 137.2 J.

5. A particle of mass 4 kg increases its speed from 3 m/s to 9 m/s. Find its change in kinetic energy.
Show answer

ΔKE = ½ × 4 × (9² − 3²).

ΔKE = 144 J.

6. A resistance of 20 N acts through 15 m. Find the energy dissipated.
Show answer

E = Rs = 20 × 15.

E = 300 J.

7. A 5 kg particle falls 4 m from rest. Ignore resistance. Find its speed at the bottom.
Show answer

mgh = ½mv².

v² = 2gh = 2 × 9.8 × 4 = 78.4.

v ≈ 8.85 m/s.

8. A motor does 45 000 J of work in 15 s. Find its average power.
Show answer

P = W/t = 45000/15.

P = 3000 W.

EXAM TRAPS

Common Mistakes to Eliminate

Mistake Correct approach
Using W = Fs for every force. Use W = Fs cosθ when the force is not parallel to displacement.
Using slope distance as h. Use the vertical height change.
Making resistance positive in a work equation. Resistance opposes motion, so its work is negative.
Including normal reaction work unnecessarily. If reaction is perpendicular to displacement, its work is zero.
Using P = Fv when F and v are not parallel. Use P = Fv cosθ.
Confusing energy and power. Energy is measured in J; power is measured in W.
Using initial speed instead of final speed. Write ½mu² and ½mv² explicitly before substituting.
Forgetting resistance energy. Add Rs as energy dissipated.
Using height instead of vertical height difference. Use Δh.
Forgetting that work can be negative. Consider the direction of the force relative to displacement.

EXAM METHOD

How to Attack a Work & Energy Question

Identify the states. Write down the initial and final speeds and heights.
Look for energy information. Speeds, heights, distances, resistance and work strongly suggest an energy method.
Choose the correct model. Use work-energy for resultant work, or conservation of energy for energy transfers.
Calculate vertical height. On an incline, h = s sinθ.
Account for resistance. Constant resistance over distance s transfers Rs of energy.
Account for applied forces. A driving force may supply energy through work.
Keep signs physically meaningful. Gravity and resistance can remove kinetic energy.
Check the result. A speed must be non-negative, efficiency cannot exceed 100% in an ordinary passive machine, and units must agree.
High-level exam strategy

Do not automatically use Newton's second law just because the question contains forces. If the question connects force/work with displacement, speed or height, an energy method may be significantly shorter.
