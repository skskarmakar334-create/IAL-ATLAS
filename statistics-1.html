TOPIC 01 // STATISTICAL SAMPLING

Populations, Samples and Sampling

A statistical investigation begins by identifying the population about which information is required. In many real investigations it is impractical, expensive or impossible to collect data from every member of the population. A sample is therefore selected.

Population

The population is the complete set of individuals or objects about which the investigation is concerned.

EXAMPLE

If a school wants to investigate the average number of hours its students spend studying each week, the population could be all students in that school.

Sample

A sample is a subset of the population selected for observation. Statistical conclusions about the population are made using information obtained from the sample.

Census

A census collects information from every member of the population. Although a census eliminates sampling variation, it may be expensive, time-consuming or difficult to organise.

Sampling Frame

A sampling frame is a list or system identifying the members of the population from which the sample is selected.

Simple Random Sampling

In simple random sampling, every possible sample of the required size has an equal chance of being selected. Random numbers can be used to select individuals from a sampling frame.

ADVANTAGE: Simple random sampling reduces deliberate selection by the researcher and gives members of the population an equal opportunity of selection.
LIMITATION: A complete sampling frame is required, and a purely random sample may still be unrepresentative by chance.

Systematic Sampling

Systematic sampling selects members at regular intervals from a sampling frame. If the population contains N members and n are required, the sampling interval is approximately N/n.

Sampling interval ≈ N ÷ n

A random starting point is normally chosen before applying the fixed interval.

Stratified Sampling

Stratified sampling divides the population into relevant strata and selects a sample from each stratum in proportion to the size of that stratum.

Sample from stratum = (stratum population ÷ total population) × total sample size

WORKED EXAMPLE // STRATIFIED SAMPLE

A college contains 600 Year 12 students and 400 Year 13 students. A sample of 50 students is required.

Year 12 sample = (600/1000) × 50 = 30

Year 13 sample = (400/1000) × 50 = 20

Therefore the stratified sample contains 30 Year 12 and 20 Year 13 students.

Quota Sampling

Quota sampling sets required numbers for categories of individuals. The interviewer then selects individuals until each quota is filled. Unlike stratified random sampling, the selection within each category is not necessarily random.

Opportunity Sampling

Opportunity sampling selects individuals who are readily available. It is usually convenient but can produce substantial bias because the people available may differ systematically from the wider population.

Sampling Bias

Bias occurs when the method of collecting data systematically favours some members or characteristics of the population over others.

Non-response

Non-response occurs when selected individuals do not provide the required information. If non-responders differ from responders, the resulting sample may be biased.

Response Bias

Response bias can occur when individuals give inaccurate answers, perhaps because a question is sensitive, leading or difficult to understand.

Designing a Good Sample

  • Define the population clearly.
  • Use an appropriate sampling frame.
  • Choose a sampling method suitable for the investigation.
  • Reduce opportunities for selection bias.
  • Use an adequate sample size.
  • Consider non-response and measurement issues.
  • Ensure the wording of questions is neutral.

TOPIC 02 // DATA REPRESENTATION

Types of Data and Statistical Diagrams

Qualitative Data

Qualitative data describes categories or qualities rather than numerical measurements. Examples include eye colour, transport type and brand.

Quantitative Data

Quantitative data is numerical. It can be divided into discrete and continuous data.

Discrete Data

Discrete data can take separate, countable values. Examples include number of children in a family and number of defective components.

Continuous Data

Continuous data can take any value within an interval. Measurements such as height, mass, time and temperature are commonly continuous.

Primary and Secondary Data

  • Primary data: collected directly for the investigation.
  • Secondary data: obtained from an existing source.

Frequency Tables

A frequency table records how many observations occur in each category or class.

Grouped Data

When there are many numerical observations, values can be grouped into class intervals. Grouping makes data easier to display but loses some information because the exact values are no longer known.

Class Boundaries

For continuous measurements rounded to a stated accuracy, class boundaries may be needed. For example, values recorded to the nearest integer as 10, 11, 12 may correspond to boundaries 9.5, 10.5, 11.5 and so on.

Histograms

A histogram is used for continuous grouped data. The area of each bar is proportional to the frequency.

Frequency density = Frequency ÷ Class width

Therefore:

Frequency = Frequency density × Class width
COMMON ERROR: The height of a histogram bar represents frequency density, not necessarily frequency. Only when all class widths are equal can bar heights be directly compared as frequencies.

Stem-and-Leaf Diagrams

A stem-and-leaf diagram preserves the original observations while displaying their distribution. A key is essential.

EXAMPLE

A key such as 3 | 7 = 37 tells the reader how to interpret the diagram.

Scatter Diagrams

A scatter diagram displays paired observations for two variables. It can be used to investigate whether a linear relationship appears to exist.

Outliers

An outlier is an observation that lies unusually far from the main body of the data. An outlier should not automatically be deleted. Its cause should be investigated.

Interpreting Diagrams

When interpreting a diagram, consider centre, spread, shape, skewness, clusters, gaps and unusual observations.

TOPIC 03 // LOCATION AND SPREAD

Measures of Location and Variation

Arithmetic Mean

x̄ = Σx ÷ n

The mean uses every observation and is therefore sensitive to extreme values.

Mean from a Frequency Table

x̄ = Σfx ÷ Σf

For grouped data, class midpoints are commonly used to estimate the mean.

Estimated mean = Σ(f × midpoint) ÷ Σf
IMPORTANT: A mean calculated from grouped data is generally an estimate because the exact observations inside each class are unknown.

Median

The median is the central observation after the data has been arranged in order. It is less affected by extreme observations than the mean.

Mode

The mode is the value or category occurring most frequently.

Range

Range = Maximum − Minimum

The range measures the total spread but depends only on two observations.

Quartiles

The lower quartile Q₁ divides approximately the lowest quarter of the ordered data from the upper three quarters. Q₃ similarly separates the upper quarter.

Interquartile Range

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁

The IQR measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data and is less affected by extreme values than the range.

Variance

Variance = E(X²) − [E(X)]²

For a dataset, variance measures the average squared distance from the mean. Its units are the square of the original units.

Standard Deviation

Standard deviation = √Variance

Standard deviation is expressed in the same units as the original data and gives a measure of typical spread around the mean.

Effect of Transformations

If every observation is increased by a constant a, the mean increases by a but the variance and standard deviation remain unchanged.

If Y = X + a: E(Y) = E(X) + a Var(Y) = Var(X)

If every observation is multiplied by a constant b, the mean is multiplied by b, the variance is multiplied by b², and the standard deviation is multiplied by |b|.

If Y = bX: E(Y) = bE(X) Var(Y) = b²Var(X)

Coding

Coding can simplify calculations by replacing x with a transformed variable such as y = (x − a)/b. The original statistics can then be recovered using the transformation.

WORKED EXAMPLE // CODING

Suppose y = (x − 50)/10 and the mean of y is 2.4.

x = 10y + 50

Therefore:

mean(x) = 10(2.4) + 50

mean(x) = 74

TOPIC 04 // PROBABILITY FIELD

Probability and Events

Probability provides a numerical measure of how likely an event is to occur.

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1

A probability of 0 represents an impossible event and a probability of 1 represents a certain event.

Sample Space

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.

Complement

P(A′) = 1 − P(A)

Union

A ∪ B means that A or B or both occur.

Intersection

A ∩ B means that both A and B occur.

Addition Rule

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

The intersection is subtracted because it is counted twice when P(A) and P(B) are added.

Mutually Exclusive Events

Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot occur together.

P(A ∩ B) = 0

Therefore:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

Conditional Probability

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B)

The notation P(A|B) means the probability of A occurring given that B has already occurred.

Independent Events

Events A and B are independent if the occurrence of one does not alter the probability of the other.

P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)

For independent events:

P(A|B) = P(A)

Tree Diagrams

Tree diagrams can be used to represent sequences of events. Probabilities along a path are multiplied, while probabilities of alternative paths are added.

EXAM METHOD: On a tree diagram, multiply along branches and add separate successful paths.

Venn Diagrams

Venn diagrams are useful for visualising unions, intersections, complements and mutually exclusive events.

TOPIC 05 // DISCRETE RANDOM VARIABLES

Discrete Random Variables and Probability Distributions

A random variable assigns a numerical value to the outcome of a random experiment.

Discrete Random Variable

A discrete random variable can take a countable set of possible values. For example, X may represent the number of defective items in a sample.

Probability Distribution

A probability distribution lists the possible values of X together with their probabilities.

ΣP(X = x) = 1

Every probability must satisfy:

0 ≤ P(X = x) ≤ 1

Finding an Unknown Probability

If a distribution contains an unknown constant k, use the fact that the probabilities must add to 1.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Suppose probabilities are k, 2k, 3k and 4k.

k + 2k + 3k + 4k = 1

10k = 1

k = 0.1

Expected Value

E(X) = ΣxP(X = x)

The expected value is the long-run average value of X over a large number of repetitions of the experiment.

Expected Value of a Function

For suitable functions of X:

E[g(X)] = Σg(x)P(X = x)

Variance

Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]²

Standard Deviation

σ = √Var(X)

Linear Transformations

E(aX+b) = aE(X)+b
Var(aX+b) = a²Var(X)

Adding a constant changes location but not spread. Multiplication changes both location and spread.

TOPIC 06 // BINOMIAL ENGINE

Binomial Distribution

The binomial distribution models the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials when each trial has two outcomes and the probability of success remains constant.

X ~ B(n,p)

The Four Conditions

  1. There is a fixed number n of trials.
  2. Each trial has exactly two relevant outcomes: success or failure.
  3. The probability of success p is constant.
  4. The trials are independent.
MODEL CHECK: Do not automatically use the binomial distribution simply because a question contains repeated trials. All four conditions must be justified.

Probability of Exactly r Successes

P(X = r) = C(n,r)pʳ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ʳ

Combination Notation

C(n,r) = n! ÷ [r!(n−r)!]

At Least

“At least r” means r or more.

P(X ≥ r) = P(X=r)+P(X=r+1)+...+P(X=n)

Often the complement is easier:

P(X ≥ r) = 1 − P(X ≤ r−1)

At Most

P(X ≤ r) = P(X=0)+...+P(X=r)

More Than

P(X > r) = 1 − P(X ≤ r)

Less Than

P(X < r) = P(X ≤ r−1)

Mean

E(X) = np

Variance

Var(X) = np(1−p)

Standard Deviation

σ = √[np(1−p)]

WORKED EXAMPLE // BINOMIAL

A machine produces components with probability 0.1 of being defective. Ten components are selected independently. Let X be the number of defective components.

X ~ B(10,0.1)

P(X=2)=C(10,2)(0.1)²(0.9)⁸

E(X)=np=10(0.1)=1

Var(X)=np(1-p)=10(0.1)(0.9)=0.9

When Binomial Is Not Appropriate

  • The number of trials is not fixed.
  • There are more than two relevant outcomes.
  • The probability of success changes from trial to trial.
  • The trials are not independent.

TOPIC 07 // CORRELATION AND REGRESSION

Correlation, Product Moment and Regression

Bivariate Data

Bivariate data consists of paired observations for two variables. For example, height and mass measured for the same group of people.

Scatter Diagram

A scatter diagram can reveal the direction, strength and approximate form of a relationship between two variables.

Positive Correlation

As one variable increases, the other tends to increase.

Negative Correlation

As one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.

No Linear Correlation

There may be little evidence of a linear relationship. This does not necessarily mean that no relationship of any kind exists.

Product Moment Correlation Coefficient

The product moment correlation coefficient, commonly denoted by r, measures the strength and direction of linear association.

−1 ≤ r ≤ 1

Values close to 1 indicate strong positive linear correlation. Values close to −1 indicate strong negative linear correlation. Values close to 0 indicate weak linear correlation.

IMPORTANT: Correlation does not establish causation. Two variables can be strongly correlated without one causing the other.

Regression Line

A regression line provides a model for predicting one variable from another.

y = a + bx

Here a is the intercept and b is the gradient.

Interpolation

Interpolation predicts a value within the range of observed x-values. It is generally more defensible than extrapolation because it remains within the observed region.

Extrapolation

Extrapolation predicts beyond the observed range. The linear model may not remain valid outside the available data.

Residuals

A residual is the difference between an observed value and the value predicted by the regression model.

Residual = observed y − predicted y

Interpreting r

The sign of r gives the direction of linear correlation, while the magnitude of r indicates its strength.

Correlation and Causation

A strong correlation can arise because one variable causes another, because both are influenced by another variable, because of coincidence, or because of the way the data was collected.

EXAM SYSTEM // ESSENTIAL TECHNIQUE

How to Approach S1 Questions

1. Identify the Statistical Structure

Before calculating, identify whether the question involves sampling, summary statistics, probability, a random variable, binomial modelling, or correlation/regression.

2. Define Variables

When using a probability model, define X clearly. For example: “Let X be the number of defective components.”

3. State the Model

For binomial questions write:

X ~ B(n,p)

This demonstrates that you have identified the correct distribution.

4. Keep Full Precision

Do not round intermediate values unnecessarily. Round only at the appropriate stage, normally at the final answer.

5. Interpret in Context

A statistical answer should often be interpreted using the original context. Do not leave an answer as a disconnected numerical value when the question asks for a conclusion.

6. Check Plausibility

  • Probability must lie between 0 and 1.
  • Standard deviation cannot be negative.
  • Variance cannot be negative.
  • A probability distribution must sum to 1.
  • A correlation coefficient must lie between −1 and 1.
  • Expected values do not necessarily have to be possible individual outcomes.

100. A factory produces components independently with probability 0.08 of a component being defective. Twenty-five components are selected. Define a suitable random variable, state its distribution, find its expected value and variance, and write an expression for the probability that at least three components are defective.
SHOW ANSWER

Let X be the number of defective components.

X~B(25,0.08)

E(X)=25(0.08)=2

Var(X)=25(0.08)(0.92)=1.84

P(X≥3)=1−P(X≤2)

So:

P(X≥3)=1−[P(X=0)+P(X=1)+P(X=2)]
