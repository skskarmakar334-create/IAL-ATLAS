Lesson 1 — What is accounting?

Accounting identifies, records, summarises and communicates financial information so users can make decisions.

Who uses accounting information?

  • Owners: profitability, risk and return.
  • Managers: planning, control and resource allocation.
  • Lenders: ability to repay loans and interest.
  • Suppliers: whether credit will be paid on time.
  • Employees: stability, wages and prospects.
  • Government: taxation and regulatory compliance.
  • Customers: continuity of supply, especially for long-term contracts.

Key accounting concepts

  • Business entity: the business is accounted for separately from its owner.
  • Going concern: assume the business will continue operating unless evidence suggests otherwise.
  • Accruals: record income and expenses in the period they relate to.
  • Consistency: use methods consistently to aid comparison.
  • Prudence: use caution where estimates are uncertain; do not overstate assets or profit.
  • Materiality: focus on information that could affect decisions.
  • Money measurement: record items that can be measured in monetary terms.
Important distinction: profit is not the same as cash. A credit sale may increase profit before the customer pays; buying equipment may reduce cash now but its cost is normally spread over its useful life through depreciation.

Lesson 2 — The accounting equation

Assets = Capital (Equity) + Liabilities

Assets are resources controlled by the business. Liabilities are amounts owed to others. Capital/equity is the owner's residual interest after liabilities are deducted.

CategoryExamplesTypical effect
Non-current assetsEquipment, vehicles, buildingsUsed over more than one accounting period
Current assetsInventory, trade receivables, bank, cashExpected to be realised/used in the operating cycle or near term
Current liabilitiesTrade payables, overdraft, short-term accrualsDue in the near term
Non-current liabilitiesLong-term loanDue after more than one year, subject to classification rules
EquityCapital + profit − drawingsOwner's residual claim

Lesson 3 — Debit and credit

Debit (Dr) means the left side of an account; credit (Cr) means the right side. Neither word automatically means “good/bad”, “increase/decrease” or “money in/out”. The effect depends on the account type.

Account typeIncreaseDecrease
AssetsDebitCredit
Expenses and drawingsDebitCredit
LiabilitiesCreditDebit
Capital/equityCreditDebit
Income/revenueCreditDebit
Memory aid: Assets, Expenses and Drawings usually increase on the debit side. Liabilities, Capital and Income usually increase on the credit side.

Lesson 5 — Books of prime entry and ledger

Books of prime entry

First records of transactions: sales journal/day book, purchases journal/day book, sales returns journal, purchases returns journal, cash book, petty cash book and general journal.

Ledger accounts

Transactions are posted to individual accounts. Balance an account by comparing its two sides; the balance is carried down and brought down to the next period as appropriate.

Trial balance

A list of ledger balances used to check whether total debit balances equal total credit balances. Agreement is useful but does not prove every transaction is correct.

Errors not revealed by a trial balance: complete omission, error of principle, error of commission where equal amounts are posted to the wrong personal account, compensating errors, and reversal of entries. Some one-sided posting errors can cause disagreement.

Lesson 6 — Inventory and cost of sales

Cost of sales = Opening inventory + Net purchases + Carriage inwards − Closing inventory

Net purchases normally equal purchases less purchases returns. Carriage inwards is a cost of bringing purchased goods to the business; carriage outwards is a selling/distribution expense. Closing inventory is deducted from goods available for sale because it has not yet been sold.

Inventory valuation is generally at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV), subject to the applicable reporting framework. NRV is estimated selling price less costs to complete and sell. Common cost formulas include FIFO and weighted average where permitted by the relevant rules.

Lesson 7 — Income statement

Revenue − Cost of sales = Gross profit
Gross profit + Other income − Expenses = Profit for the period

The income statement reports performance over a period. Revenue is recognised when earned under the applicable rules, not necessarily when cash is received. Expenses include rent, wages, insurance, depreciation and irrecoverable debts. Drawings are not an expense in the income statement.

Lesson 8 — Statement of financial position

This statement shows assets, liabilities and equity at a specific date.

Closing capital = Opening capital + Additional capital + Profit − Drawings

Present non-current assets, current assets, equity, non-current liabilities and current liabilities using the format required by your course. The statement must balance because assets equal equity plus liabilities.

Lesson 9 — Accruals and prepayments

Accrued expense

An expense relates to this period but has not yet been paid. Add it to the expense in the income statement and show a current liability.

Expense = Cash paid + Closing accrual − Opening accrual

Prepaid expense

Cash was paid but some benefit relates to a future period. Deduct the closing prepayment from the current period expense and show a current asset.

Expense = Cash paid + Opening prepayment − Closing prepayment

Accrued income

Income earned but not yet received: add to income and recognise a receivable asset.

Income received in advance

Cash received for income not yet earned: deduct the unearned part from income and recognise a liability.

Lesson 10 — Depreciation

Depreciation allocates the depreciable amount of a non-current asset over its useful life. It is not a direct valuation of the asset's market price and is not a cash payment in the period.

Straight-line annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life
Reducing-balance depreciation = Net book value at start of year × Rate

Accumulated depreciation is the total depreciation charged to date. Net book value (carrying amount) is usually cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment. A full-year convention, part-year convention and residual-value treatment should follow the question's instructions.

Lesson 11 — Irrecoverable debts and allowance

An irrecoverable (bad) debt is written off when it is judged not recoverable. A doubtful-debt allowance estimates expected losses on remaining receivables. The expense for the period is generally the required closing allowance minus the existing allowance, adjusted for any specific write-offs as the method requires.

Net trade receivables = Trade receivables − Allowance for receivables

Example: receivables are 20,000 and a 5% allowance is required. Closing allowance = 20,000 × 5% = 1,000. If the existing allowance is 700, the increase charged as an expense is 300.

Lesson 12 — Bank reconciliation

A bank reconciliation explains differences between the cash book bank balance and the bank statement balance. Common timing differences include unpresented cheques and deposits not yet credited. Bank charges, direct debits, standing orders and bank interest shown by the bank but not yet recorded should first be entered in the cash book.

Do not automatically add every difference: decide which record is missing the item and whether the item increases or decreases that balance. Follow the exact starting balance and sign convention in the question.

Lesson 13 — Control accounts and suspense

Control accounts summarise totals from subsidiary ledgers and help locate errors. A receivables control account checks the total owed by credit customers; a payables control account checks the total owed to credit suppliers. A suspense account temporarily holds an unexplained difference when a trial balance does not agree. It should be cleared once errors are identified and corrected.

Lesson 14 — Manufacturing accounts

Prime cost = Direct materials + Direct labour + Direct expenses
Factory cost = Prime cost + Production overheads + Opening WIP − Closing WIP

Work in progress (WIP) is partly completed production. Production overheads include indirect factory costs such as factory rent and indirect labour. Administrative and selling expenses are normally period expenses, not production costs, unless the specific framework or question says otherwise.

Lesson 15 — Cost behaviour and break-even

Contribution per unit = Selling price per unit − Variable cost per unit
Break-even output = Total fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit
Margin of safety = Actual/budgeted output − Break-even output
Profit = Total contribution − Fixed costs

Fixed costs stay constant in total within a relevant range; variable costs change in total with output; semi-variable costs contain fixed and variable elements. Break-even assumes selling price, variable cost per unit and total fixed costs behave predictably over the relevant range, and that output equals sales for the simple model.

Lesson 16 — Budgets and variances

A budget is a quantified plan for a future period. It helps planning, coordination, communication and control. A variance is the difference between actual and budgeted performance. “Favourable” and “adverse” depend on the effect on profit or the objective: lower-than-budget cost is often favourable, while lower-than-budget revenue is often adverse. Always explain the reason, not just the label.

Lesson 18 — Ethics and limitations

Professional behaviour includes integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, and professional conduct. Ethical threats can arise from personal interests, pressure from management, familiarity or conflicts of interest. Accounting estimates require judgement; accounts can be affected by policies, inflation, seasonality, one-off events and window dressing. Ratios should be compared across time and similar businesses, not interpreted in isolation.

Worked examples

Follow the calculation line by line. Try to explain why each number is included or excluded.

Example A — Accounting equation

A business has assets of 85,000 and liabilities of 31,000.

Capital = Assets − Liabilities
= 85,000 − 31,000
= 54,000

Check: 54,000 + 31,000 = 85,000.

Example B — Cost of sales

Opening inventory 8,000; purchases 42,000; purchases returns 2,000; carriage inwards 1,000; closing inventory 9,500.

Net purchases = 42,000 − 2,000 = 40,000
Cost of sales = 8,000 + 40,000 + 1,000 − 9,500
= 39,500

Example C — Accrued rent

Cash rent paid during the year is 12,000. Closing rent accrual is 1,500 and opening accrual is 500.

Rent expense = 12,000 + 1,500 − 500 = 13,000

The closing accrual is a liability; the opening accrual was settled during this year and is subtracted from cash paid to calculate this year's expense.

Example D — Straight-line depreciation

Equipment costs 30,000, residual value is 3,000 and useful life is 6 years.

Annual depreciation = (30,000 − 3,000) ÷ 6
= 4,500 per year

Example E — Break-even

Selling price 50 per unit, variable cost 30 per unit, fixed costs 12,000.

Contribution = 50 − 30 = 20
Break-even = 12,000 ÷ 20 = 600 units

At 700 units sold: profit = (700 × 20) − 12,000 = 2,000.

Example F — Liquidity ratios

Current assets 24,000; inventory 9,000; current liabilities 12,000.

Current ratio = 24,000 ÷ 12,000 = 2:1
Acid-test = (24,000 − 9,000) ÷ 12,000
= 1.25:1

Whether this is healthy depends on the industry, cash cycle and quality of receivables/inventory.
