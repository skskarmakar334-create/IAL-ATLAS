01 / The Fundamentals

What is Economics?

Economics studies how individuals, businesses and governments allocate limited resources to satisfy competing needs and wants.

1. Scarcity

Resources are limited, but human wants are unlimited. This fundamental problem forces people and societies to make choices.

Example: If you have ৳500, you may have to choose between buying a textbook and going out with friends.

2. Choice and Opportunity Cost

Every choice involves giving something up. Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone when a decision is made.

Example: If studying for an exam means missing a football match, the match may be your opportunity cost.

3. The Four Factors of Production

  • Land: Natural resources, such as oil and soil.
  • Labour: Human effort used in production.
  • Capital: Man-made resources used to produce goods and services.
  • Enterprise: Organising production and taking business risks.

4. The Three Basic Economic Questions

  • What goods and services should be produced?
  • How should they be produced?
  • For whom should they be produced?

Different economic systems answer these questions in different ways.

5. Microeconomics

Microeconomics examines individual markets and decision-makers: consumers, workers and firms.

Example: How does the price of rice affect the quantity consumers buy?

6. Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics examines the economy as a whole, including economic growth, inflation, unemployment and international trade.

Example: Why might a country's general price level rise?
02 / Essential Concepts

Build Your Economics Foundation

Learn these concepts before moving into more advanced economic analysis.

Demand

Demand is the quantity of a good or service consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices over a given period.

  • When price falls, quantity demanded usually rises.
  • When price rises, quantity demanded usually falls.
  • This is the law of demand, other things being equal.
Price ↑ → Quantity demanded ↓

A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve. Changes in income, tastes or the prices of related goods can shift the demand curve.

Supply

Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to sell at different prices over a given period.

  • Higher prices usually encourage producers to supply more.
  • Lower prices usually encourage producers to supply less.
  • Production costs and technology can shift supply.
Price ↑ → Quantity supplied ↑

A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the supply curve. A change in production costs can shift the whole curve.

Market Equilibrium

Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. The market-clearing price is the equilibrium price.

Quantity demanded = Quantity supplied
  • Shortage: Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
  • Surplus: Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.

Elasticity

Elasticity measures how responsive one variable is to a change in another.

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
  • PED greater than 1 in absolute value: Elastic demand.
  • PED less than 1 in absolute value: Inelastic demand.
  • PED equal to 1 in absolute value: Unit elastic demand.

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how responsive quantity supplied is to a change in price.

Market Failure

Market failure occurs when the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently from society's perspective.

  • Externalities: Effects on third parties.
  • Public goods: Non-rival and non-excludable goods.
  • Information gaps: Buyers or sellers lack relevant information.
  • Market power: Firms may restrict competition.

GDP and Economic Growth

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) measures the value of final goods and services produced within a country during a given period.

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

C = consumption, I = investment, G = government spending, X = exports and M = imports.

Real GDP adjusts for changes in the general price level, helping economists assess changes in output.

Inflation and Unemployment

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level. It reduces the purchasing power of money when incomes do not rise correspondingly.

Unemployment occurs when people who are willing and able to work cannot find a job.

Common types of unemployment include frictional, structural and cyclical unemployment.

Fiscal and Monetary Policy

Governments and central banks can influence economic activity through different policy tools.

  • Fiscal policy: Government spending and taxation.
  • Monetary policy: Interest rates and other monetary tools.

Expansionary policies aim to support economic activity; contractionary policies aim to reduce excess demand or inflationary pressure.
