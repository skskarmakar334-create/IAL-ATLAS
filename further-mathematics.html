01 / The Foundation

Welcome to Further Mathematics

Further Mathematics extends the techniques and ideas studied in Mathematics. Exact content depends on your qualification and exam board, so use these notes as a general foundation rather than a complete specification checklist.

01. Complex Numbers

A complex number has a real part and an imaginary part. The imaginary unit is defined by \(i^2=-1\).

z = a + bi

Here, a is the real part and b is the coefficient of the imaginary part.

Example: If z = 3 + 2i and w = 1 − 4i, then z + w = 4 − 2i.

For multiplication, expand normally and replace every \(i^2\) with −1.

02. Modulus and Argument

A complex number can be represented on an Argand diagram, with the real part on the horizontal axis and the imaginary part on the vertical axis.

|z| = √(a² + b²)
z = r(cos θ + i sin θ)

The modulus r is the distance from the origin. The argument θ is the angle measured from the positive real axis, taking the correct quadrant into account.

Example: For z = 3 + 4i, |z| = √(9 + 16) = 5.

03. Matrices

Matrices organise numbers into rows and columns. You can add matrices of the same dimensions and multiply compatible matrices.

A = [ a b ]
[ c d ]

For a 2 × 2 matrix, its determinant is:

det(A) = ad − bc
Example: If A = [[2, 1], [3, 4]], then det(A) = 2 × 4 − 1 × 3 = 5.

A 2 × 2 matrix has an inverse only when its determinant is non-zero.

04. Vectors

A vector has magnitude and direction. In two dimensions, a vector can be written using its horizontal and vertical components.

a = (x, y)
|a| = √(x² + y²)

Vectors can be added component by component. Scalar multiplication scales each component.

Example: (2, 3) + (4, −1) = (6, 2).

05. Differentiation

Differentiation finds the rate of change of a function. It is used to study gradients, stationary points and optimisation.

If y = xⁿ, then dy/dx = nxⁿ⁻¹
Example: If y = 3x⁴, then dy/dx = 12x³.

At a stationary point, dy/dx = 0. Further analysis can help determine whether it is a maximum, minimum or another type of stationary point.

06. Integration

Integration can reverse differentiation and can calculate areas under curves when the appropriate conditions are met.

∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1) + C, n ≠ −1
Example: ∫ 6x² dx = 2x³ + C.

The constant C is needed for an indefinite integral because differentiating a constant gives zero.

07. Sequences and Series

An arithmetic sequence has a constant difference between consecutive terms.

uₙ = a + (n − 1)d

A geometric sequence has a constant ratio between consecutive terms.

uₙ = arⁿ⁻¹
Example: For 5, 8, 11, 14, ... the common difference is 3 and the fifth term is 17.

08. Mathematical Proof

Proof uses logical reasoning to establish that a statement is true under its stated assumptions.

  • Direct proof: Start from known facts and derive the result.
  • Proof by contradiction: Assume the opposite and derive a contradiction.
  • Induction: Prove a base case and show one case implies the next.
A few successful examples are not enough to prove a statement true for every positive integer.

09. Binomial Expansion

Binomial expansion expresses a power of a sum as a series of terms.

(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
(a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³

For larger positive integer powers, binomial coefficients can be arranged using Pascal's triangle or calculated with combinations.

10. Differential Equations

A differential equation connects an unknown function with one or more of its derivatives.

dy/dx = ky

One solution is y = Aeᵏˣ, where A is a constant determined by an initial condition.

Example: If dy/dx = 2y, then y = Ae²ˣ satisfies the equation.

11. Numerical Methods

Numerical methods approximate solutions when exact algebraic solutions are difficult or unavailable.

Newton–Raphson: xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − f(xₙ)/f′(xₙ)

The method uses a starting estimate and tangent-line information to generate a new estimate of a root.

The starting value matters; convergence is not guaranteed for every function and initial estimate.

12. Mathematical Modelling

A mathematical model represents a real-world situation using variables, equations and assumptions.

  • Define the variables and assumptions.
  • Construct the mathematical relationship.
  • Solve and interpret the result in context.
  • Check whether the answer is realistic.
A model is only useful when its assumptions and limitations are understood.
