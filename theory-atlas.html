Extended Theory

Projectile Motion

Explain why the horizontal and vertical components of the motion of a projectile can be treated independently.
A projectile moving freely under gravity has two perpendicular components of motion. Horizontally, if air resistance is neglected, there is no resultant horizontal force, so the horizontal velocity remains constant. Vertically, the projectile experiences a downward gravitational acceleration g. The horizontal motion therefore determines how far the projectile travels horizontally, while the vertical motion determines its vertical position. The two components occur simultaneously but can be analysed separately and then combined to describe the complete motion.
Key marking points:
Horizontal acceleration is zero · horizontal velocity is constant · vertical acceleration is g downward · components are independent · both motions occur simultaneously
Extended Theory

Newton's Laws and Equilibrium

Explain how Newton's laws can be used to analyse an object that is at rest or moving at constant velocity.
Newton's second law states that the resultant force on an object is equal to its mass multiplied by its acceleration. If the object is at rest or moving with constant velocity, its acceleration is zero. Therefore the resultant force is zero. The forces acting on the object must consequently balance. A free-body diagram can be used to identify all the forces acting on the object and their directions. Resolving forces into suitable directions allows the equilibrium conditions to be checked quantitatively.
Key marking points:
ΣF = ma · a = 0 · resultant force = 0 · forces balance · appropriate force diagram/resolution
Extended Theory

Conservation of Momentum

Explain the principle of conservation of linear momentum and how it can be applied to collisions or interactions.
Momentum is the product of mass and velocity. In an isolated system, where no resultant external force acts, the total momentum remains constant. For an interaction between objects, the total momentum before the interaction equals the total momentum after the interaction. Momentum must be treated as a vector quantity, so a chosen positive direction must be used consistently when solving one-dimensional problems.
Key marking points:
p = mv · isolated system · no resultant external force · total momentum conserved · direction/sign convention
Extended Theory

Principle of Moments

Explain how the principle of moments can be used to determine whether an extended rigid body is in equilibrium.
The moment of a force about an axis is the force multiplied by the perpendicular distance between the line of action of the force and the axis. For an extended rigid body in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any chosen axis equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments. The centre of gravity must also be considered because the weight of the body acts through its centre of gravity.
Key marking points:
Moment = force × perpendicular distance · clockwise moments · anticlockwise moments · equilibrium · centre of gravity
Extended Theory

Energy Conservation

Explain how kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy and work done are related by conservation of energy.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transferred between different stores or forms. For a system near the Earth's surface, gravitational potential energy can be transferred into kinetic energy as an object moves downward. Work done by forces can also transfer energy into or out of the system. If dissipative effects are absent, the total mechanical energy remains constant.
Key marking points:
Energy conserved · gravitational potential energy · kinetic energy · work done represents energy transfer · dissipative effects where relevant

Stokes' Law and Terminal Velocity

Explain how a small spherical object falling through a viscous liquid can reach terminal velocity.
As the sphere begins to fall, its weight acts downward while the upthrust and viscous drag act upward. Initially the sphere accelerates because the downward resultant force is non-zero. As its speed increases, the viscous drag increases. Eventually the upward forces balance the weight. The resultant force then becomes zero, so acceleration becomes zero and the sphere continues at a constant terminal velocity. Stokes' law applies to small spherical objects moving at low speeds where the flow is laminar.
Key marking points:
Weight downward · upthrust upward · drag increases with speed · forces balance · resultant force zero · acceleration zero · constant terminal velocity · conditions for Stokes' law
Extended Theory

Hooke's Law and Elastic Behaviour

Explain Hooke's law and distinguish between elastic and plastic deformation.
Hooke's law states that the extension of an object is proportional to the applied force provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded. In the proportional region, increasing the force by a given factor produces the same factor increase in extension. When an object is elastically deformed, it returns to its original dimensions when the force is removed. If the material is plastically deformed, it does not fully return to its original dimensions.
Key marking points:
ΔF = kΔx · proportional region · limit of proportionality · elastic deformation · plastic deformation
Extended Theory

Stress, Strain and Young Modulus

Explain the relationship between stress, strain and Young modulus for a material.
Stress is the applied force per unit cross-sectional area, while strain is the change in length divided by the original length. Young modulus is the ratio of stress to strain. A material with a high Young modulus requires a relatively large stress to produce a given strain and is therefore relatively stiff. The behaviour of the material can be investigated using a stress-strain graph.
Key marking points:
Stress = force/area · strain = change in length/original length · Young modulus = stress/strain · stiffness · stress-strain graph
Extended Theory

Force-Extension Graph

Explain how a force-extension graph can be used to describe the behaviour of a material.
Initially, the force may be proportional to extension, giving a straight-line section through the origin. The end of this region is the limit of proportionality. If the material is stretched further, the graph may become non-linear. The elastic limit represents the greatest deformation from which the material can return to its original shape. Beyond the elastic region, permanent plastic deformation may occur. The material may eventually reach a point where it breaks.
Key marking points:
Linear proportional region · limit of proportionality · elastic limit · plastic deformation · breaking point/stress

S1 Theory

Scalar Quantity

What is a scalar quantity?
A scalar quantity has magnitude only and no direction.
S1 Theory

Vector Quantity

What is a vector quantity?
A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction.
S1 Theory

Centre of Gravity

What is the centre of gravity of an extended body?
The centre of gravity is the point through which the weight of an extended body may be considered to act.
S1 Theory

Newton's First Law

State Newton's first law of motion.
An object remains at rest or continues to move with constant velocity unless acted upon by a resultant external force.
S1 Theory

Newton's Third Law

State Newton's third law of motion.
When two bodies interact, they exert forces on each other that are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
S1 Theory

Momentum

Define momentum.
Momentum is the product of an object's mass and velocity.
S1 Theory

Terminal Velocity

What is terminal velocity?
Terminal velocity is the constant velocity reached when the resultant force on a falling object becomes zero.
S1 Theory

Upthrust

State the relationship between upthrust and displaced fluid.
The upthrust on an object is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object.
S1 Theory

Viscosity

What is viscosity?
Viscosity is a measure of a fluid's resistance to flow. A more viscous fluid provides greater resistance to motion through it.
S1 Theory

Limit of Proportionality

What is the limit of proportionality?
It is the point up to which extension remains directly proportional to applied force.
S1 Theory

Elastic Deformation

What is elastic deformation?
Elastic deformation is deformation from which a material returns to its original shape and dimensions when the applied force is removed.
S1 Theory

Plastic Deformation

What is plastic deformation?
Plastic deformation is permanent deformation that remains after the applied force is removed.
Other Theory · Important Understanding
Other Theory

Displacement-Time Graph

What does the gradient of a displacement-time graph represent?
The gradient represents velocity. A steeper gradient corresponds to a greater magnitude of velocity, while a horizontal line represents zero velocity.
Other Theory

Velocity-Time Graph

What do the gradient and area under a velocity-time graph represent?
The gradient represents acceleration. The area under the graph represents displacement.
Other Theory

Acceleration-Time Graph

What does the area under an acceleration-time graph represent?
The area under an acceleration-time graph represents the change in velocity.
Other Theory

Free-Body Diagram

What is the purpose of a free-body diagram?
A free-body diagram represents the forces acting on a selected object. It helps identify the magnitude and direction of forces before applying Newton's laws or resolving forces.
Other Theory

Conditions for Stokes' Law

Under what conditions can Stokes' law be applied?
Stokes' law applies to a small spherical object moving at low speed through a fluid where the flow is laminar rather than turbulent. Viscosity also depends on temperature.
Other Theory

Elastic Strain Energy

How can elastic strain energy be found from a force-extension graph?
The elastic strain energy stored is equal to the area under the force-extension graph. For a linear relationship, this area is a triangle and gives ½FΔx. For a non-linear graph, the area can be estimated.
