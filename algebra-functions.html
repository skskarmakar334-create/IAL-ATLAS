01 / FOUNDATIONS

Learn to read algebra

Before solving complicated questions, you need to understand exactly what every symbol and structure is saying.

1.1 The basic vocabulary

Core

An expression is a mathematical object made from numbers, variables and operations. For example, 3x² − 5x + 7 is an expression.

An equation states that two expressions are equal. For example, 3x² − 5x + 7 = 0.

An identity is an equality that is true for every permitted value of the variables. For example:

(x + 3)² ≡ x² + 6x + 9

The symbol ≡ is important. It communicates that the two sides are algebraically identical, rather than merely equal for one particular value of x.

Term

A term is a part of an expression separated by plus or minus signs.

4x² − 7x + 3

The terms are 4x², −7x and 3.

Coefficient

The coefficient is the numerical multiplier of a variable.

7x² → coefficient = 7

The coefficient of x in −5x is −5.

Constant

A constant contains no variable.

2x² + 9x + 14

Here 14 is the constant term.

Factor

A factor is something multiplied by something else.

12x² = 3 × 4 × x × x

3, 4, x and x are factors of the product.

Core distinction
Do not confuse an expression with an equation. You simplify expressions; you solve equations.
Worked example 01.01
Question: Identify the terms, coefficients and constant in 5x² − 3x + 11.
Step 1: Separate the expression at the plus/minus signs.
5x²    −3x    +11
Step 2: The terms are 5x², −3x and 11.
Step 3: The coefficient of x² is 5.
Step 4: The coefficient of x is −3.
Step 5: The constant is 11.
Answer: terms = 5x², −3x, 11; coefficients = 5 and −3; constant = 11.
Checkpoint: If you see 8x + 3x² − 4, you should immediately identify three terms: 8x, 3x² and −4.

1.2 Like terms

Essential

Like terms have exactly the same variable part, including the same powers of every variable.

3x² and −7x² → like terms

4xy and 9xy → like terms

5x and 5x² → NOT like terms

The reason you can combine like terms is that they represent the same quantity multiplied by different numerical coefficients.

Worked example 01.02
Question: Simplify 7x² − 4x + 3x² + 9x − 11.
Step 1: Group x² terms: 7x² + 3x² = 10x².
Step 2: Group x terms: −4x + 9x = 5x.
Step 3: Keep the constant: −11.
10x² + 5x − 11
Common mistake: You cannot combine x² and x. For example, x² + x does NOT become 2x² or 2x.

1.3 Order of operations

Foundation

Algebra follows the same basic hierarchy of operations as arithmetic. Brackets are evaluated first, then powers/roots, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.

Brackets → Indices → Multiplication/Division → Addition/Subtraction

This becomes particularly important when a negative number is raised to a power.

−3²

The square applies to 3 before the negative sign.

−3² = −9

(−3)²

The brackets make the entire negative number the base.

(−3)² = 9
02 / MANIPULATION

Algebraic manipulation

Manipulation means changing the form of an expression without changing its mathematical value.

2.1 The distributive law

Core rule

Multiplication distributes across addition and subtraction:

a(b + c) = ab + ac

a(b − c) = ab − ac

This is the mathematical basis of expanding brackets.

Worked example 02.01
Question: Expand 5(2x − 3).
Step 1: Multiply 5 by every term inside the bracket.
5(2x − 3) = 5(2x) − 5(3) = 10x − 15
Answer: 10x − 15
Sign trap: When the number outside a bracket is negative, every term inside the bracket changes sign after multiplication.

2.2 Expanding two brackets

Essential

When multiplying two binomials, every term in the first bracket must multiply every term in the second bracket.

(a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd
Worked example 02.02
Question: Expand (x + 4)(x + 7).
Step 1: Multiply x by both terms: x·x = x² and x·7 = 7x.
Step 2: Multiply 4 by both terms: 4·x = 4x and 4·7 = 28.
x² + 7x + 4x + 28
Step 3: Collect like terms.
x² + 11x + 28
Answer: x² + 11x + 28
Harder example 02.03
Question: Expand (2x − 3)(x + 5).
2x(x) + 2x(5) − 3(x) − 3(5)
2x² + 10x − 3x − 15
2x² + 7x − 15
Answer: 2x² + 7x − 15

2.3 Important identities

Memorise + understand

An identity is especially useful because it allows you to move quickly between expanded and factorised forms.

(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²

(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²

(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²
Recognising an identity 02.04
Question: Expand (x − 6)².
Use (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b².
x² − 2(x)(6) + 6²
x² − 12x + 36
Answer: x² − 12x + 36
Classic error: (a + b)² is NOT a² + b². The middle term 2ab is essential.

2.4 Factorisation

Reverse expansion

Factorisation is essentially expansion in reverse. You search for a common factor or a product structure that recreates the original expression.

Common factor

3x² + 12x = 3x(x + 4)

Quadratic factorisation

x² + 9x + 20 = (x + 4)(x + 5)

The numbers 4 and 5 multiply to 20 and add to 9.

Non-monic quadratic 02.05
Question: Factorise 2x² + 7x + 3.
We need two binomials whose first coefficients multiply to 2 and whose constant terms multiply to 3.
(2x + 1)(x + 3)
(2x + 1)(x + 3) = 2x² + 6x + x + 3 = 2x² + 7x + 3
Answer: (2x + 1)(x + 3)
03 / FRACTIONS

Algebraic fractions

Fractions containing variables require the same arithmetic ideas as ordinary fractions, but you must also track restrictions on the variables.

3.1 Simplifying algebraic fractions

Core

The safest approach is to factorise the numerator and denominator first. Only then should you cancel common factors.

6x²y / 3xy = (3xy)(2x) / (3xy) = 2x

The cancellation assumes the original denominator is non-zero. Therefore x ≠ 0 and y ≠ 0 for the original expression.

Never cancel across addition. For example: (x + 3) / x cannot be simplified by cancelling the x with part of x + 3.
Correct cancellation 03.01
(x² − 9)/(x + 3)
Factorise the numerator.
[(x − 3)(x + 3)]/(x + 3)
Now cancel the common factor x + 3.
x − 3
But the original expression requires x + 3 ≠ 0, so:
x ≠ −3

3.2 Solving equations with fractions

Exam skill

A useful method is to multiply every term by the lowest common denominator. This removes fractions while preserving equality.

Worked example 03.02
Question: Solve 3/x = 6.
Multiply both sides by x, provided x ≠ 0.
3 = 6x
x = 1/2
Answer: x = 1/2
04 / INDICES

Indices

Indices compress repeated multiplication and create a bridge between algebraic powers, roots and surds.

4.1 Positive indices

Foundation

For a positive integer n:

aⁿ = a × a × a × ... × a

with n copies of a.

2⁵ = 2×2×2×2×2 = 32

4.2 Multiplication law

Rule 01
aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ

When multiplying powers with the same base, add the indices.

Example 04.01
2³ × 2⁴ = 2³⁺⁴ = 2⁷
The bases must match. You cannot turn 2³ × 3⁴ into 6⁷.

4.3 Division law

Rule 02
aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
Example 04.02
7⁶ / 7² = 7⁴

4.4 Power of a power

Rule 03
(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
Example 04.03
(3²)⁴ = 3⁸
Do not add the indices here. The outside power multiplies the inside power.

4.5 Zero and negative indices

Important
a⁰ = 1    (a ≠ 0)

a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ
Examples 04.04
5⁻² = 1/5² = 1/25
x⁻³ = 1/x³
9⁰ = 1

4.6 Fractional indices

Advanced core
a¹⁄ⁿ = ⁿ√a

aᵐ⁄ⁿ = ⁿ√(aᵐ)
Worked examples 04.05
16¹⁄² = √16 = 4
27²⁄³ = (∛27)² = 3² = 9
9⁻¹⁄² = 1/√9 = 1/3
Fractional index questions become much easier when you translate them into roots first, calculate the root, and then apply the remaining power.
05 / SURDS

Surds

Surds are exact irrational quantities. The goal is not to turn them into decimals, but to manipulate them into clean exact forms.

5.1 Simplifying square roots

Core

Look for the largest perfect-square factor inside the radical.

Worked examples 05.01
√50 = √(25×2) = 5√2
√72 = √(36×2) = 6√2
√48 = √(16×3) = 4√3

5.2 Adding and subtracting surds

Like surds

Only like surds can be collected, just as only like algebraic terms can be collected.

Worked example 05.02
√12 + √27
Simplify each surd.
2√3 + 3√3
5√3
Answer: 5√3

5.3 Multiplying surds

Core
√a × √b = √(ab)
Example 05.03
√3 × √12 = √36 = 6

5.4 Rationalising a single denominator

Exam skill

A rational denominator contains no irrational radical. Multiply numerator and denominator by the same surd.

Worked example 05.04
1/√5
Multiply by √5/√5.
√5/(√5×√5) = √5/5
Answer: √5/5

5.5 Rationalising binomial surds

Higher difficulty

When the denominator contains a sum or difference of two terms, use its conjugate.

(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²
Worked method 05.05
Suppose the denominator is √3 + √2.
Its conjugate is √3 − √2.
1/(√3 + √2) × (√3 − √2)/(√3 − √2)
= (√3 − √2)/(3 − 2)
= √3 − √2
Answer: √3 − √2
The denominator becomes rational because the middle terms cancel. That cancellation is exactly why the conjugate is useful.
06 / QUADRATICS

Quadratic equations

Quadratics connect algebra, equations, graphs, roots, parameters and inequalities. Mastering them is one of the biggest gains in algebra.

6.1 What makes an equation quadratic?

Definition

A quadratic equation can be written in the form:

ax² + bx + c = 0

a ≠ 0

The highest power of x is 2.

x² − 5x + 6 = 0

Here a = 1, b = −5 and c = 6.

6.2 Solving by factorisation

Method 01
Worked example 06.01
Question: Solve x² − 5x + 6 = 0.
Find two numbers whose product is 6 and whose sum is −5.
−2 and −3
(x − 2)(x − 3) = 0
A product is zero only when at least one factor is zero.
x − 2 = 0   or   x − 3 = 0
x = 2 or x = 3

6.3 Difference of squares

Fast method
a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
Example 06.02
x² − 49 = x² − 7² = (x − 7)(x + 7)

6.4 Quadratic formula

Universal method
x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a)

This works for any quadratic equation in the standard form ax² + bx + c = 0.

Worked example 06.03
Solve 2x² − 3x − 2 = 0.
Identify: a = 2, b = −3, c = −2.
x = [−(−3) ± √((−3)² − 4(2)(−2))]/(4)
x = [3 ± √(9 + 16)]/4
x = (3 ± 5)/4
x = 2   or   x = −1/2

6.5 The discriminant

Parameter tool

The discriminant is:

Δ = b² − 4ac
Discriminant Number of real roots Graph interpretation
Δ > 0 Two distinct real roots Crosses x-axis twice
Δ = 0 One repeated real root Touches x-axis once
Δ < 0 No real roots Does not meet x-axis
Parameter example 06.04
For x² + 6x + k = 0 to have equal roots, what is k?
Equal roots require Δ = 0.
6² − 4(1)(k) = 0
36 − 4k = 0
k = 9
Answer: k = 9

6.6 Completing the square

Graph power

Completing the square rewrites a quadratic so that its turning point, minimum/maximum and axis of symmetry become immediately visible.

x² + 10x + 21 = (x + 5)² − 4

Therefore:

Turning point = (−5, −4)
Axis of symmetry: x = −5
General structure 06.05
y = a(x − h)² + k

The turning point is:

(h,k)

If a > 0, the graph has a minimum. If a < 0, the graph has a maximum.

6.7 Roots and coefficients

Relationship

For:

ax² + bx + c = 0

If the roots are α and β, then:

α + β = −b/a

αβ = c/a

These relationships can save a lot of algebra when a question asks for a sum or product of roots rather than the roots themselves.

6.8 Quadratic inequalities

Important

First find the roots. Then determine where the quadratic is positive or negative.

Worked example 06.06
Solve x² − 9 > 0.
x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3)
The roots are −3 and 3.
Since the coefficient of x² is positive, the graph is above the x-axis outside the roots.
x < −3   or   x > 3
Answer: x < −3 or x > 3
Second example 06.07
x² − 4x + 3 ≤ 0
(x − 1)(x − 3) ≤ 0
1 ≤ x ≤ 3
07 / SYSTEMS

Simultaneous equations

Two equations can be solved together because the same unknown values must satisfy both equations simultaneously.

7.1 Elimination

Method 01
Worked example 07.01
x + y = 7
x − y = 1
Add the equations.
2x = 8
x = 4
Substitute into x + y = 7.
4 + y = 7
y = 3
Solution: (x,y) = (4,3)

7.2 Substitution

Method 02

If one equation easily gives x or y in terms of the other variable, substitution is usually efficient.

y = 6 − x

Substitute this into the second equation.

7.3 Linear and quadratic systems

Higher difficulty

A common structure is:

y = 2x + 1
y = x² − 5

Because both expressions equal y, set them equal to each other:

2x + 1 = x² − 5

This produces a quadratic equation. The resulting x-values can then be substituted back to find the corresponding y-values.

Graphically, the solutions are the intersection points of the two graphs. The algebra and the graph are describing the same event from two different perspectives.
08 / INEQUALITIES

Inequalities

Inequalities describe ranges of possible values rather than a single solution.

8.1 Linear inequalities

Core
Worked example 08.01
3x + 2 > 11
3x > 9
x > 3
Answer: x > 3

8.2 Multiplying or dividing by a negative

Critical rule

When multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number, the inequality sign reverses.

Worked example 08.02
−4x > 20
x < −5

The sign changes from > to < because we divided by −4.

8.3 Number-line thinking

Interpretation

x > 3

3 is excluded and values to the right are included.

(3,∞)

x ≥ 3

3 is included and values to the right are included.

[3,∞)
09 / FUNCTIONS

Functions

Functions turn an input into exactly one output. They are the language behind much of modern mathematics.

9.1 Function notation

Foundation

Suppose:

f(x) = 2x + 5

Then f(x) means the output of the function when the input is x.

Evaluation 09.01
f(3) = 2(3) + 5 = 11
Therefore f(3) = 11.

9.2 Finding an input from an output

Reverse thinking
Worked example 09.02
f(x) = 3x − 4
Find x when f(x) = 11.
3x − 4 = 11
3x = 15
x = 5

9.3 Domain and range

Essential

The domain is the set of permitted inputs. The range is the set of outputs produced.

Square root

f(x) = √x

For real-valued outputs:

x ≥ 0

Reciprocal

f(x) = 1/(x−2)

The denominator cannot be zero:

x ≠ 2

9.4 Composite functions

Core

A composite function means that the output of one function becomes the input of another.

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x))
Worked example 09.03
f(x) = x + 2
g(x) = 3x
Find f(g(4)).
First calculate g(4).
g(4) = 12
Now put 12 into f.
f(12) = 14
Therefore f(g(4)) = 14.
Order matters. In general, f(g(x)) is not equal to g(f(x)).

9.5 Algebraic composite functions

Exam skill
Worked example 09.04
f(x) = 2x + 1
g(x) = x − 4
Find f(g(x)).
f(g(x)) = 2(x − 4) + 1
= 2x − 8 + 1
= 2x − 7
Answer: f(g(x)) = 2x − 7

9.6 One-to-one functions

Inverse preparation

A function is one-to-one if different inputs produce different outputs. Equivalently, no horizontal line should intersect the graph more than once.

x²

The function x² is not one-to-one on all real numbers because:

f(2) = 4
f(−2) = 4

Two different inputs produce the same output.

However, x² can become one-to-one if its domain is restricted, for example to x ≥ 0.

9.7 Inverse functions

Advanced core

An inverse function reverses the action of the original function.

f⁻¹(f(x)) = x

f(f⁻¹(x)) = x
Worked example 09.05
f(x) = 3x + 2
Start with:
y = 3x + 2
Swap x and y.
x = 3y + 2
Solve for y.
x − 2 = 3y
y = (x − 2)/3
Therefore:
f⁻¹(x) = (x − 2)/3

9.8 Restrictions and inverses

Important

Some functions are not one-to-one on their natural domain. In those cases, a domain restriction may be necessary before an inverse can be defined as a function.

Quadratic example 09.06
f(x) = x²

On all real numbers, x² is not one-to-one.

If we restrict:

x ≥ 0

then the function becomes one-to-one and its inverse can be:

f⁻¹(x) = √x
10 / GRAPHS

Functions and graph transformations

Changing the formula changes the graph in predictable ways. Learn the pattern instead of memorising isolated examples.

10.1 Vertical translation

Graph rule
y = f(x) + a

Moves the graph vertically by a units.

a > 0 → upward
a < 0 → downward

10.2 Horizontal translation

Graph rule
y = f(x − a)

Moves the graph to the right by a units.

The sign inside the bracket often feels backwards. If you see x − 3, the graph moves right 3, not left 3.

10.3 Reflection

Graph rule
y = −f(x)

Reflects the graph in the x-axis.

y = f(−x)

Reflects the graph in the y-axis.

10.4 Stretching

Graph rule
y = af(x)

This is a stretch parallel to the y-axis with scale factor |a|.

y = f(ax)

This produces a horizontal scale effect with scale factor 1/|a|.

11 / MODELLING

Algebra in context

A model translates a real situation into mathematical structure. The hardest part is often deciding what the variables actually mean.

11.1 Turning words into algebra

Reasoning

Suppose a rectangle has width x and length 3x + 2.

Area = x(3x + 2)
A = 3x² + 2x

The algebra is not the final answer until you check whether the variable values make physical sense.

Model discipline
Always ask: What does x represent? What values are physically possible? What units should the final answer have? Does the answer make sense in the original context?

11.2 Rejecting impossible solutions

Exam reasoning

An algebraic equation may produce multiple mathematical solutions, but the context may allow only some of them.

Example 11.01

If x represents a physical length and solving the equation gives:

x = 7 or x = −2

The negative solution may be mathematically valid for the equation but physically invalid for a length.

Context determines which mathematical solutions are meaningful.
12 / REASONING

Algebraic proof

Proof questions require a chain of statements where each transformation can be justified.

12.1 Showing an identity

Proof

To prove two expressions are identical, transform one side into the other using valid algebraic operations.

Example 12.01
(x + 2)² − (x − 2)²
= (x² + 4x + 4) − (x² − 4x + 4)
= x² + 4x + 4 − x² + 4x − 4
= 8x
Therefore the identity is proved.
13 / ERROR DATABASE

Common mistakes

Most lost marks are not caused by impossible mathematics. They are caused by small structural errors.

01 — Squaring brackets

(a+b)² ≠ a²+b²

The missing 2ab term changes the entire expression.

02 — Cancelling terms

(x+3)/x ≠ 3

Cancellation only applies to common factors.

03 — Negative powers

x⁻² = 1/x²

A negative index means reciprocal, not negative value.

04 — Inequality sign

−2x > 6 → x < −3

Dividing by a negative reverses the sign.

05 — Composite order

f(g(x)) ≠ g(f(x))

The inner function acts first.

06 — Quadratic formula

−b ± √(b²−4ac)

The entire numerator belongs over 2a.

07 — Discriminant

b²−4ac

Do not forget that b may itself be negative.

08 — Surd collection

√2 + √3

These cannot be combined because they are unlike surds.
