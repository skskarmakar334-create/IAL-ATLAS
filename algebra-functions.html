Learning Architecture

Algebra becomes much easier when every operation has a reason. This page therefore builds the topic from notation and structure, through manipulation and equations, into functions and finally exam-level reasoning.

00 • Before You Start

How to Think About Algebra

Core idea Algebra is not primarily about symbols. It is about relationships between quantities. The letters are simply compact representations of quantities that may be unknown, variable, fixed or constrained.

1. Expression

An expression is a mathematical object such as 3x + 7, x² − 4x + 1 or (x + 2)/(x − 5). An expression does not contain an equality sign.

2. Equation

An equation states that two expressions have equal value. For example:

3x + 7 = 22

Solving the equation means finding the values of x for which the statement is true.

3. Identity

An identity is true for every permitted value of the variable.

(x + 3)² ≡ x² + 6x + 9 The symbol ≡ means identically equal.

4. Inequality

Inequalities compare quantities without requiring them to be equal:

x > 4    x ≤ 7    x ≠ 2

5. Terms, coefficients and constants

Object Example Meaning
Term 7x² A single algebraic part separated by + or −.
Coefficient 7 in 7x² The numerical multiplier.
Variable x A quantity whose value may vary.
Constant 12 A fixed value.
Factor 3 and x in 3x Quantities multiplied together.
Worked Example

Identify the coefficient of x² in:

−8x² + 5x − 11
The x² term is −8x².
Therefore its coefficient is −8.
Common trap: The coefficient of x² in x² is 1, not 0 and not x.
Checkpoint: Before manipulating an expression, identify what type of object you are looking at: expression, equation, identity or inequality.
01 • Algebraic Foundations

Like Terms, Simplification & Structure

Like terms

Two terms are like terms when their variable parts are identical. Their numerical coefficients may be different.

7x + 3x = 10x

However:

7x + 3x² ≠ 10x³

The powers of x are different, so these terms cannot be combined.

Worked Example

Simplify:

5x + 8 − 2x + 3 − 4x
Group x terms:
5x − 2x − 4x = −x
Group constants:
8 + 3 = 11
Therefore:
−x + 11

Removing brackets

The distributive law says:

a(b + c) = ab + ac

Every term inside the bracket must be multiplied.

Worked Example
5(2x − 3) − 2(x + 4)
5(2x − 3) = 10x − 15
−2(x + 4) = −2x − 8
Combine:
10x − 15 − 2x − 8
= 8x − 23

Nested brackets

When brackets occur inside other brackets, work systematically from the innermost structure outward.

Challenge Example
3[2x − (x − 4)]
First simplify inside:
2x − (x − 4)
The minus sign changes both terms:
2x − x + 4 = x + 4
Multiply by 3:
3x + 12
Major trap: When a negative sign is immediately before a bracket, every sign inside that bracket changes.

Algebraic simplification checklist

Remove brackets carefully.
Collect only like terms.
Keep signs attached to terms.
Do not combine unlike powers.
Check the result by substituting a simple value.
02 • Identities

Algebraic Identities & Special Products

Difference of two squares

a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
Example
x² − 49

Recognise 49 as 7²:

x² − 7²
(x − 7)(x + 7)

Perfect square identities

(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
Expansion
(3x − 5)²
(3x)² − 2(3x)(5) + 5²
= 9x² − 30x + 25

Recognising perfect squares

Suppose we have:

x² + 12x + 36

Since 36 = 6² and 12 = 2 × 6, this is:

(x + 6)²
Exam lens: Recognition is often faster than multiplying out and attempting factorisation from scratch.
03 • Factorisation

Factorising Algebraic Expressions

Common factor

Always check for a common factor before trying more complicated methods.

6x² + 15x = 3x(2x + 5)

Quadratic factorisation

For a monic quadratic:

x² + bx + c

look for two numbers whose product is c and whose sum is b.

Worked Example
x² + 9x + 20
Numbers multiplying to 20 and adding to 9:
4 and 5.
Therefore:
(x + 4)(x + 5)

Quadratics with a coefficient other than 1

2x² + 7x + 3

Multiply the first and last coefficients:

2 × 3 = 6

Find two numbers multiplying to 6 and adding to 7:

6 and 1

Split the middle term:

2x² + 6x + x + 3

Group:

2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3)

Therefore:

(2x + 1)(x + 3)

Factorisation as the reverse of expansion

Expansion turns products into sums. Factorisation reverses this process. That is why multiplying your factors back together is an excellent checking method.

Trap: Do not stop after finding a common factor if the remaining expression can still be factorised.
04 • Algebraic Fractions

Fractions, Restrictions & Rational Expressions

Basic simplification

6x²y / 3xy = 2x

Cancellation is based on factors, not individual terms.

Factor before cancelling

Important Example
(x² − 9)/(x + 3)

Factor the numerator:

(x − 3)(x + 3)/(x + 3)

Therefore:

x − 3

But there is an important restriction:

x ≠ −3
Important: Even after cancellation, the original denominator restriction remains.

Adding algebraic fractions

Fractions must have a common denominator before their numerators can be added.

Worked Example
1/x + 2/(x + 1)

Common denominator:

x(x + 1)
1/x = (x + 1)/[x(x + 1)]
2/(x + 1) = 2x/[x(x + 1)]
Therefore:
(3x + 1) / [x(x + 1)]

Restrictions:

x ≠ 0, −1
05 • Indices

Index Laws From First Principles

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
(ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ
(a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ

Why multiplication adds powers

Consider:

x³ × x²

Expanded:

(x × x × x)(x × x) = x⁵

Therefore:

x³ × x² = x⁵

Zero index

a⁰ = 1    (a ≠ 0)

Negative indices

a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ
Example
5⁻² = 1/5² = 1/25

Fractional indices

a¹/² = √a
a¹/³ = ∛a
aᵐ/ⁿ = ⁿ√(aᵐ)
Example
27²/³
Cube root of 27 = 3.
3² = 9.

Combining everything

Challenge
x⁵/² × x⁻¹/²
Add powers:
5/2 − 1/2 = 4/2 = 2
Therefore:
x²
06 • Surds

Exact Values, Simplification & Rationalisation

What is a surd?

A surd is an exact irrational expression involving a root that cannot be simplified to a rational number.

√2, √3, √5, 2√7

Extracting square factors

Example
√72
72 = 36 × 2
√72 = √36 × √2
= 6√2

Collecting surds

√12 + √27
√12 = 2√3
√27 = 3√3
Therefore:
5√3

Multiplication

√3 × √12 = √36 = 6

Rationalising a denominator

Single Surd
1/√5

Multiply numerator and denominator by √5:

√5 / 5

Conjugates

For a denominator such as:

a + √b

use its conjugate:

a − √b

because:

(a + √b)(a − √b) = a² − b
Challenge
1/(3 + √2)
Multiply by the conjugate:
(3 − √2)/(3 − √2)
Denominator:
(3 + √2)(3 − √2) = 9 − 2 = 7
Therefore:
(3 − √2)/7
07 • Quadratic Equations

Roots, Formula, Discriminant & Structure

What is a quadratic?

ax² + bx + c = 0    a ≠ 0

The highest power of the variable is 2.

Method 1 — Factorisation

Example
x² − 5x + 6 = 0
(x − 2)(x − 3) = 0

A product is zero when at least one factor is zero:

x − 2 = 0 → x = 2
x − 3 = 0 → x = 3

Method 2 — Quadratic formula

x = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)] / 2a

This works for any quadratic equation in the standard form.

The discriminant

Δ = b² − 4ac

Δ > 0

Two distinct real roots.

Δ = 0

One repeated real root.

Δ < 0

No real roots.

Completing the square

Worked Example
x² + 10x + 21
Half of 10 is 5.
Add and subtract 25:
x² + 10x + 25 − 25 + 21
(x + 5)² − 4

Turning point

y = (x − h)² + k

The turning point is:

(h,k)
Example
y = (x − 3)² + 2

Therefore the turning point is:

(3,2)

Because the coefficient of the squared bracket is positive, this is a minimum.

Axis of symmetry

For:

y = ax² + bx + c

the axis is:

x = −b/(2a)
Example
y = x² − 6x + 1
a = 1, b = −6
x = −(−6)/(2)
x = 3

Relationship between roots and coefficients

For:

ax² + bx + c = 0

with roots α and β:

α + β = −b/a
αβ = c/a
Exam lens: If a question asks for a sum or product of roots, you may not need to solve the quadratic at all.
08 • Simultaneous Equations

Linear Systems & Linear–Quadratic Systems

Substitution

Example
x + y = 7
x − y = 1
Add the equations:
2x = 8
x = 4
Substitute:
4 + y = 7
y = 3

Elimination

Example
2x + y = 9
x + y = 6
Subtract the second equation from the first:
x = 3
3 + y = 6
y = 3

Geometric meaning

Two linear equations represent two straight lines. Their solution is the intersection point.

One solution

The lines intersect once.

No solution

The lines are parallel.

Infinitely many

The equations represent the same line.

Linear and quadratic equations

When one equation is linear and the other quadratic, substitution often turns the system into a quadratic equation.

Structure
y = 2x + 1
y = x² − 3

Set the right sides equal:

2x + 1 = x² − 3

Rearrange:

x² − 2x − 4 = 0

Solve for x, then substitute into either original equation to find y.

09 • Inequalities

Linear & Quadratic Inequalities

Linear inequalities

3x + 2 > 11
3x > 9
x > 3

The reversal rule

If you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, the inequality sign reverses.

Example
−4x > 20
Divide by −4.
The sign reverses:
x < −5

Quadratic inequalities

Example
x² − 9 > 0
Factor:
(x − 3)(x + 3) > 0
Critical values:
x = −3, 3
The quadratic is positive outside the roots.
x < −3 or x > 3

Quadratic below or equal to zero

Example
x² − 4x + 3 ≤ 0
Factor:
(x − 1)(x − 3) ≤ 0
The parabola lies below the x-axis between the roots.
1 ≤ x ≤ 3
Exam lens: For a quadratic inequality, find the roots first. Then determine the sign in each interval.
10 • Functions

Function Notation, Domain & Range

What is a function?

A function assigns each allowed input exactly one output.

f : x ↦ 2x + 5

This can also be written:

f(x) = 2x + 5

Evaluating a function

Example
f(x) = 2x + 5

Find f(3).

f(3) = 2(3) + 5
f(3) = 11

Solving f(x) = a

Example
f(x) = 3x − 4

Find x when f(x) = 11.

3x − 4 = 11
3x = 15
x = 5

Domain

The domain is the set of permitted input values.

For:

f(x) = √x

we require:

x ≥ 0

Therefore the domain is:

[0,∞)

Restricted domains

For:

f(x) = 1/(x − 2)

x = 2 is not permitted because division by zero is undefined.

Domain: x ≠ 2

Range

The range is the set of output values produced by the function over its domain.

Example
f(x) = x²

For real x:

x² ≥ 0

Therefore:

Range: y ≥ 0
11 • Composite Functions

Composition & Order

Meaning of composition

The notation:

fg(x)

usually means:

f(g(x))

So g acts first and f acts second.

Worked Example
f(x) = x + 2
g(x) = 3x

Find f(g(4)).

g(4) = 12
f(12) = 14
f(g(4)) = 14

Algebraic composition

Example
f(x) = 2x + 1
g(x) = x − 4

Find f(g(x)).

Replace x in f with g(x):
f(g(x)) = 2(x − 4) + 1
= 2x − 7
Order matters: f(g(x)) and g(f(x)) are generally different functions.
12 • Inverse Functions

Finding & Understanding f⁻¹

What does an inverse do?

A function sends an input to an output. Its inverse reverses that mapping.

f⁻¹(f(x)) = x

and, where appropriately defined:

f(f⁻¹(x)) = x

Finding an inverse

Worked Example
f(x) = 3x + 2

Write:

y = 3x + 2

Swap x and y:

x = 3y + 2

Solve for y:

x − 2 = 3y
y = (x − 2)/3
f⁻¹(x) = (x − 2)/3

Why x² needs a restriction

The function:

f(x) = x²

is not one-to-one over all real numbers because:

f(2) = 4
f(−2) = 4

Two different inputs produce the same output. Therefore an inverse function cannot exist over the unrestricted real domain.

If the domain is restricted to x ≥ 0, then:

f⁻¹(x) = √x

Graphical meaning

The graph of a function and its inverse are reflections of each other in the line:

y = x
13 • Function Transformations

Moving & Reshaping Graphs

Vertical translation

y = f(x) + a

moves the graph upward by a units when a is positive.

Horizontal translation

y = f(x − a)

moves the graph to the right by a units.

Reflection in x-axis

y = −f(x)

Reflection in y-axis

y = f(−x)

Vertical stretch

y = af(x)

changes the vertical scale by factor |a|.

Horizontal scale

y = f(ax)

produces a horizontal scaling by factor 1/|a|.

Important Sign Check

Starting with:

y = x²

The graph:

y = (x − 4)² + 3

has moved:

  • 4 units right
  • 3 units up

So its turning point is:

(4,3)
14 • Modelling

Turning Real Situations Into Algebra

Mathematical modelling means translating a real situation into variables, equations, functions or inequalities.

A reliable modelling sequence

Define the variable clearly.
Translate the information into algebra.
Solve the mathematical model.
Reject values that violate the situation.
Interpret the answer using units and context.
Check whether the result is realistic.
Example

A rectangle has width x and length x + 5. Its area is 84.

x(x + 5) = 84
x² + 5x − 84 = 0
(x + 12)(x − 7) = 0

Therefore:

x = −12 or x = 7

A physical width cannot be negative, so:

x = 7

Width = 7 and length = 12.

Exam lens: Algebra can produce mathematically valid solutions that are impossible in the real situation. Always interpret the final answer.
